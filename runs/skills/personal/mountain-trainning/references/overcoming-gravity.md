# Overcoming Gravity: extended agent summary

## 1. Header

### Book identity

- Title: *Overcoming Gravity: A Systematic Approach to Gymnastics and Bodyweight Strength*.
- Author: Steven Low (former gymnast and coach).
- Edition: second edition. Copyright year 2016. Publisher: Battle Ground Creative (Houston).
- First edition: 2011. The second edition aims to be simpler and easier to teach.
- Source file: `../Overcoming Gravity_ ... (Second Edition)-to-markdown.md` (automatic conversion).
- This file is an original paraphrase. It does not reproduce the book's charts, tables, progression lists or sample routines.

### Scope of the book

- Main topic: building strength with the body as the load. Gymnastics-derived skills dominate: handstands, levers, planche, rings, muscle-ups, iron cross.
- Main audience: beginner to intermediate recreational athletes who want to design their own routine.
- The author wants the reader to learn to build routines, not to copy the sample routines.
- It is not a climbing, mountaineering or endurance book. Climbing appears only in short passages (see section 4.9).
- It contains no finger-strength or hangboard programme.
- It is not medical advice. The book says this repeatedly.

### How to use this file

- Read sections 3 and 4 for principles. Read section 5 for exercise families. Read sections 6 to 8 before you advise an athlete.
- Tags used in this file:
  - **[B]** = content taken from the book (paraphrased).
  - **[I]** = my inference or synthesis. The book does not say it.
  - **[B/I]** = book content with my added interpretation.
- Chapter names in brackets point to the book's contents list. The numbered `Chapter N` headings inside the conversion are unreliable (see section 9).
- Treat all numbers as the book's rules of thumb. Several come from the author's coaching practice, not from controlled studies. The book says so in places.
- Do not paste the book's wording into replies to the athlete. Explain in your own words and cite the chapter name.

## 3. Training theory

### 3.1 SAID principle and progressive overload [Ch 1]

- **[B]** SAID means the body adapts specifically to the demands placed on it. This applies to muscle, nerves and connective tissue.
- **[B]** Progressive overload means the demand must rise over time. With a barbell, the load rises. With bodyweight, the exercise must get harder in another way.
- **[B]** The book says bodyweight work can build strength and muscle in a way comparable to weights, if the difficulty keeps rising.
- **[B]** Specificity matters for fibre type. Moderate fibres follow the training they get: endurance work biases them toward endurance, heavy work toward strength.
- **[I]** Consequence for planning: always ask "what will make this harder next?" before you choose an exercise.

### 3.2 Leverage and lever manipulation [Ch 1]

- **[B]** Bodyweight cannot be added in small steps, so the book lowers the athlete's mechanical advantage.
- **[B]** Method 1: change body position. Moving mass farther from the working joint raises torque. Example: tuck to straddle to straight body in planche or lever work.
- **[B]** Method 2: change muscle length. Muscles are strongest near mid-range. Short or long positions produce a training stimulus with less force. Straight-arm work puts the biceps and shoulder muscles in a long, weak position.
- **[B]** Full range of motion is preferred. Shortened range gives more repetitions but less benefit.
- **[B]** Difficulty rises steeply as the angle decreases. In the book's V-sit example, a modest angle change roughly doubles the difficulty.
- **[I]** Leverage changes also raise tendon and joint load quickly. This is why the book gates straight-arm skills behind preparation (see 3.9).

### 3.3 Strength, skill and hypertrophy [Ch 1, 2, 5]

- **[B]** Strength is described as neural adaptation multiplied by muscle cross-section.
- **[B]** Neural factors named: recruitment, firing rate, synchronisation within a muscle, coordination between muscles, relaxation of opposing muscles, and motor learning. The book says strength is partly a skill.
- **[B]** Heavy or hard efforts recruit the high-threshold fibres that have the most strength and growth potential. The intent to move fast matters even when the movement is slow.
- **[B]** Three hypertrophy routes: mechanical tension, muscle damage from volume, and metabolic stress. The book rejects the old "sarcoplasmic vs myofibrillar" split.
- **[B]** The book says bigger muscle rarely hurts strength-to-weight outcomes for most people.
- **[B]** Skill work is low-fatigue practice of balance or positions. Handstands are skill work. Planche, levers and cross are strength work, even though they are isometric.
- **[B]** Practice quality matters. Poor-form practice teaches poor patterns.
- **[B]** Attributes (strength, hypertrophy, endurance, flexibility, mobility, skill, cardio) can be trained at different frequencies. The more intense the attribute, the more recovery it needs.

### 3.4 Strength-endurance and the repetition continuum [Ch 1, 5, 13]

- **[B]** Strength and endurance sit at opposite ends of a continuum. They cannot both be optimised at the same time. Some of both can be developed together.
- **[B]** More maximal strength raises the ceiling for endurance, because each repetition uses a smaller share of capacity.
- **[B]** Strength takes longer to build than endurance.
- **[B]** Strength-biased goals: put strength first. Endurance sports: train both together. Too much specialisation is inefficient.
- **[B]** A 4:1 (80/20) mix is suggested when one quality leads. Light aerobic work can help recovery from strength work.
- **[B]** Pure endurance goals use higher repetitions, density, ladders, circuits and intervals. The book treats them briefly.
- **[B]** Energy systems: very short efforts use phosphocreatine, moderate efforts glycolysis, longer efforts aerobic metabolism.
- **[B]** The book states low-intensity steady cardio is the best way to raise aerobic capacity. It is useful from intermediate level upward. Beginners focused on strength can skip it.
- **[I]** This book is thin on aerobic base building. For alpine or running goals, use the other books in this skill for the aerobic plan.

### 3.5 Rep ranges, sets and rest [Ch 5, 9]

- **[B]** Rough zones:
  - Strength: about 1 to 8 reps, near but not at failure, long rests (3 minutes or more).
  - Hypertrophy: about 5 to 15+ reps, often to failure, 1 to 3 minutes or more.
  - Endurance: about 15 to 20+ reps, shorter rests (30 to 90 seconds).
- **[B]** Beginners generally do well in the 5 to 15 range. Untrained beginners start higher (up to 15 to 20) to protect connective tissue.
- **[B]** Rule 1 for strength sets: stop one repetition short of failure. Do at least three sets. Make the last set close to or at failure.
- **[B]** Rule 2: aim for at least about 15 total repetitions per exercise. Strength work totals roughly 25 to 50. Hypertrophy work totals roughly 40 to 75 or more.
- **[B]** When max reps per set are very low (1 to 3), the rule breaks down. Use 4 to 10 total reps and add assisted, eccentric or isometric work.
- **[B]** Isometric dosing: one repetition is roughly equal to two seconds of hold. Use about 60 to 75 percent of your maximum hold per set. Keep sets near the 15-rep or 30-second ceiling, beyond which holds favour endurance.
- **[B]** Eccentric dosing: one repetition is roughly equal to three seconds of lowering. Use 3 to 10 second lowers, often chained in clusters.
- **[B]** Eccentrics are strongest, then isometrics, then concentrics. Being able to do several slow lowers usually predicts one full repetition.
- **[B]** Rest logic: ATP recovers about half in 30 seconds and nearly fully by 3 minutes. Strength work needs at least 2 minutes and preferably 3 to 5.
- **[B]** Tempo: the standard is one second down, no pause, explosive up, no pause (written 10X0). Hypertrophy responds similarly to other tempos if total work is similar. Slow tempos can be used to improve technique or lower injury risk.
- **[B]** Fatigue lowers quality across a session. The first exercise gets the best effort. Order exercises by priority.
- **[B]** Pairing exercises that use different muscles can shorten a session.

### 3.6 Frequency [Ch 5, 7]

- **[B]** Frequency is the main lever for faster progress in a skill. Strength is skill-like and movement-specific.
- **[B]** Beginners do best with full-body sessions two to three times a week, with about 48 hours between sessions.
- **[B]** The author argues a full-body plan gives many more practice sessions per year of a target skill than a push/pull split.
- **[B]** Handstand skill work can be done almost daily because it is low fatigue once balance is the focus.
- **[B]** Mobility can be trained daily. Flexibility can be trained often but its soreness can interfere with leg or strength days.
- **[B]** Rule of thumb for adding sessions: one extra weekly session per year of consistent training. Do not exceed about five days a week without great care.
- **[B]** "Workout factors" (hard strength, failure work) fit two to four times a week. "Recovery factors" (easy cardio, mobility, light skill) can be done more often if they leave you feeling better.

### 3.7 Deloading, recovery and overreaching [Ch 2, 5, 12, 14, 16]

- **[B]** Fitness and fatigue both rise after a session. Fatigue hides fitness. Gains show after rest.
- **[B]** Stalls can mean too little rest, not too little work. First reduce volume and check recovery. Only then add volume.
- **[B]** Eccentrics and isometrics are very taxing. Do not make them the bulk of a programme.
- **[B]** Cycles last 4 to 8 weeks, then a deload week.
- **[B]** Deload options: fewer sessions, fewer exercises, easier progressions, lower volume, or light different activity. The common version is one or two short sessions with one exercise per movement category.
- **[B]** Keep skill work and mobility during a deload. Test maxima near the end of the deload, with one rest day before the next cycle.
- **[B]** Overreaching is a temporary dip that rebounds after rest. Overtraining is a long under-recovery state that takes weeks or months to resolve.
- **[B]** Signs of overtraining: poor sleep, low appetite, constant soreness, falling performance, more injuries.
- **[B]** Beginners more often have the problem of too much volume in one session than true overtraining.
- **[B]** Sleep matters. The book suggests about 7.5 to 9 hours for most people and gives practical sleep habits.
- **[B]** Nutrition: protein about 0.7 to 1 gram per pound of bodyweight per day for athletes. Calories set weight change. Supplements are not a priority.
- **[B]** Training while ill: avoid hard sessions with fever or chest or lower-body illness. Light movement only if it leaves you feeling better.
- **[I]** For mountain athletes with large aerobic volume, count all sources of fatigue. The book's frequency rules assume the strength plan is the main load.

### 3.8 Structural balance [Ch 4, 6, 18, 21]

- **[B]** The shoulder is the key joint of the upper body. Routine design should protect it.
- **[B]** Pulling means the hands and centre of mass move toward each other. Pushing means they move apart. Each has vertical and horizontal forms.
- **[B]** Basic recipe: one vertical push, one horizontal push, one vertical pull, one horizontal pull, plus legs. Add handstand skill work and the L-sit to manna path.
- **[B]** Pairing handstand work (overhead end range) with L-sit/V-sit/manna (backward end range) trains the shoulder at both ends and works the rear shoulder.
- **[B]** Signs of imbalance: new clicking or popping, front-shoulder pain, a sense of instability, push levels far above pull levels.
- **[B]** Pushing is usually about equal to or one level above pulling on the book's charts. A gap of two or more levels suggests a weak area.
- **[B]** Elbows stay tucked in most bodyweight pressing and pulling. Wide-elbow work is a stepping stone only.
- **[B]** Horizontal pulling is described as essential for shoulder health.
- **[B]** Climbers tend to have strong pulling muscles relative to the chest. Extra push-ups and dips can balance this.
- **[B]** The book prefers a simple push/pull scheme over its first-edition complex scheme.

### 3.9 Tendon and connective tissue adaptation [Ch 2, 6, 11, 21, 25]

- **[B]** Muscle adapts faster than tendon, ligament and joint structures. Fast strength gains can outrun tissue capacity. This is the main route to overuse injury.
- **[B]** Higher repetitions at lower difficulty are used early to condition tissue. Deload weeks also let tissue recover.
- **[B]** Straight-arm work (levers, planche, cross, straight-arm presses) is the highest-risk category. Weight-trained athletes progress too fast there.
- **[B]** Goal ordering for tissue safety: back lever before front lever, both before one-arm chin-up or iron cross. Support holds, German hangs, ring dips and handstand work prepare the elbow and shoulder.
- **[B]** Previously athletic but detrained people are at high risk. Muscle returns fast and tendon lags.
- **[B]** Eccentric, high-repetition, not-to-failure work is the book's main tool for tendon rehab and prehab.
- **[I]** For a climber with heavy finger and elbow use, add the book's elbow caution to your own load count. The book does not cover finger tendons or pulleys.

### 3.10 Warm-up [Ch 8, 17]

- **[B]** Three parts: blood flow, mobility, position drills. Skill work follows.
- **[B]** Blood flow: easy full-body movement until there is a light sweat, mild heart-rate rise and deeper breathing. Burpees and crawling are the author's examples.
- **[B]** Mobility: joint circles, squats, support holds with straight arms, German hangs. Start passive (for example wrist rocking on all fours) if joints are stiff.
- **[B]** Position drills: planks, side planks, reverse plank, hollow and arch holds.
- **[B]** Then use two to three levels easier versions of the day's main exercise.
- **[B]** Warm-up length: about 5 to 15 minutes plus the athlete's age divided by four. Older or injured athletes need more.
- **[B]** Avoid long static stretches before strength work unless a range limit blocks the skill.
- **[B]** Second edition moved general mobility into the warm-up. Static stretching and prehab sit at the end.
- **[B]** Untrained beginners may need a gentler blood-flow choice than burpees.

### 3.11 Mobility, flexibility and prehab [Ch 4, 11, 22]

- **[B]** Definitions: flexibility is range gained by relaxing the nervous system. Passive mobility moves a joint without aiming for more range. Active mobility uses new range under strength. Stability is neuromuscular re-education.
- **[B]** Flexibility methods: static stretching (about 3 to 6 holds of 30 to 60 seconds), PNF contract-relax work, and loaded stretching.
- **[B]** Stretch into mild discomfort and relax. Pain raises muscle tension and defeats the goal.
- **[B]** Track time spent at end range. Use it or lose it. Mobility can be daily.
- **[B]** Prehab goes at the end of a session so it does not fatigue stabilisers before the main work.
- **[B]** Isolation work targets weak links. Examples: biceps curls for elbow tendon load, wrist work, rotator cuff, scapular work.
- **[B]** Shoulder resources: prone Y/T/L/P raises for rear shoulder and lower trapezius, scapular push-ups, wall slides, band dislocates, German hangs.
- **[B]** Elbow resources: soft-tissue work on forearm and arm muscles, and tendon-oriented protocols.
- **[B]** Wrist resources: rice-bucket work, wrist push-ups, curls, extensor work.
- **[B]** Also covered: hips, back, contrast baths and showers, calluses and skin tears.

### 3.12 Injury management principles [Ch 15, 21]

- **[B]** The book says there is no one-size-fits-all injury plan. Individual care is required. A clinician's advice overrides the book.
- **[B]** Muscle "burn" is not injury. Soreness is delayed and not a goal. Pain is complex.
- **[B]** It prefers "remove the aggravating exercise" over "judge by pain level".
- **[B]** Four factors affect injury risk: posture, biomechanics, mobility, muscle length-tension balance.
- **[B]** Rehab order: do not aggravate, regain range, strengthen gently from all angles, fix imbalances, return to normal training.
- **[B]** Healing has inflammation, proliferation and maturation phases. Rehab work is low-intensity, high-repetition and not to failure, with slow eccentrics.
- **[B]** Return volume: restart at a reduced share of previous volume and add weekly (see conflicts in section 9).
- **[B]** Work around injury: swap to a pain-free exercise or step down progressions. Train unaffected limbs and neighbouring joints. Opposite-limb training gives a small transfer.
- **[B]** Staying active is preferred over total rest in most cases. The book contrasts a rest-first model with an early-movement model.
- **[B]** How to find a clinician: look for orthopaedic doctors or physiotherapists who treat athletes.
- **[B]** Think critically about "bad" exercises. Ask for the injury mechanism and the alternative.

## 4. Programming

### 4.1 Goals and exercise selection [Ch 3]

- **[B]** Write specific, measurable, time-bound goals. Avoid vague goals such as "improve dips".
- **[B]** Match the goal to the quality. High-rep endurance feats are not strength goals.
- **[B]** Choose one or two goals per category: skill, push, pull, legs, core, flexibility.
- **[B]** Use the progression chart to find the exercise you can do now in the chosen family. If you cannot start the chosen ladder, train a similar easier ladder first.
- **[B]** Keep a training log. Beginners often skip it and later regret it.
- **[B]** Avoid unrealistic targets, such as many repetitions of a very advanced move.
- **[B]** The author warns against changing the routine every cycle. Keep goals for two to three cycles.

### 4.2 Routine hierarchy [Ch 5, 8, 9, 11]

- **[B]** Order of blocks:
  1. Warm-up and mobility.
  2. Skill or technique work.
  3. Strength work (power, isometrics, eccentrics, main lifts).
  4. Endurance or conditioning.
  5. Prehab, isolation, flexibility, cool-down.
- **[B]** Core work now sits near the end of the strength block, not up front, because it fatigued pushing sets.
- **[B]** Basic full-body strength block: two pushes, two pulls, one or two leg exercises, one core item, and sometimes one isometric and one eccentric item.
- **[B]** Put the goal exercise first. Others follow in priority order.

### 4.3 Routine structures [Ch 7]

- **[B]** Full-body, three sessions a week: best for beginners and for skill frequency.
- **[B]** Push/pull split: usually four sessions a week. Leg work can fall on two consecutive days.
- **[B]** Upper/lower split: useful when sport work is leg-heavy. Needs more upper exercises per session.
- **[B]** Straight-arm/bent-arm split: concentrates tendon-heavy static work on one day, lowers variety of practice.
- **[B]** Push/pull/legs split: slow progress. Suited to cautious, older or hypertrophy-led athletes. The book discourages three-part splits for beginners and intermediates.
- **[B]** Isolation work suits the two ends of the spectrum: injured and elite athletes.
- **[B]** Schedule around real life. Shift workers and busy people can use other day patterns. Two-week cycles are possible.

### 4.4 Periodisation [Ch 5, 10, 14]

- **[B]** Traditional model: microcycle (about a week), mesocycle (several weeks plus deload), macrocycle (whole season).
- **[B]** Accumulation and intensification: a volume-heavy phase followed by a harder, lower-volume phase. Planned overreaching works this way.
- **[B]** Light/heavy weeks: simplest form of variation. The book recommends it before more complex schemes.
- **[B]** Daily undulating periodisation (DUP): vary reps and sets between sessions within a week. For intermediate-to-advanced athletes, not beginners or elite.
- **[B]** Conjugate and concurrent models are named for elite athletes.
- **[B]** Beginners do not need periodisation. Linear progression is enough.
- **[B]** Bodyweight DUP uses easier variants on light days and harder variants on heavy days, with weight added when needed.

### 4.5 How to build a routine (step sequence)

1. **[B]** Set written goals. Pick one or two per category.
2. **[B]** Find the current level on each ladder.
3. **[B]** Choose a template. Use full-body three times per week unless life or injury says otherwise.
4. **[B]** Choose a warm-up with blood flow, mobility and position drills.
5. **[B]** Add skill work (handstand practice).
6. **[B]** Pick strength exercises: push (vertical and horizontal), pull (vertical and horizontal), legs, core.
7. **[B]** Dose each exercise with the rules in 3.5.
8. **[B]** Add prehab and flexibility at the end. Move non-strength parts to rest days if the session is too long.
9. **[B]** Log, progress, deload every 4 to 8 weeks.
10. **[I]** Check total weekly load against sport training before you finalise the plan.

### 4.6 How to progress, when to regress [Ch 10, 12, 17]

- **[B]** Progress inside an exercise ("intra-exercise") and by moving to a harder variant ("inter-exercise").
- **[B]** Intra-exercise methods, simplest first:
  - Add load with a vest or weights.
  - Add reps across sets in small steps (linear repetition progression is the beginner default).
  - Add a rep to one set at a time (slower).
  - Take the last set to failure to test readiness.
  - Add a set (good for pulling).
  - Rest-pause, density and tempo changes.
  - Raise frequency.
- **[B]** Inter-exercise bridges when the next variant is too hard:
  - Added weight on the easier variant.
  - Assistance (bands, pulleys, partner).
  - Eccentrics of the harder variant.
  - Extra range or small momentum.
  - Modifying the easy part of the movement.
  - More sets with shorter rests.
  - Hybrid sets (one or two reps of the harder variant, then reps of the easier one).
- **[B]** Beginner progress expectation: improve most exercises nearly every session. Intermediate: every few sessions to weekly. Advanced: weekly or fortnightly.
- **[B]** Troubleshooting order: reduce volume first (recovery), then change method, then add volume.
- **[B]** Intermediate cycles: if there is no progress after four weeks, end the cycle. Do not stop at two or three weeks, because fatigue masks gains.
- **[B]** Progress can differ across exercises. Do not compare with others.
- **[B]** Step down a level or a few levels when form breaks, tendons ache, or the next level is not yet reachable at reasonable rep counts.
- **[B]** Form faults make a hold easier. Treat them as strength deficits. Regress and rebuild.
- **[B]** When joints complain at fast progress, raise repetitions (for example from 15 to 20) before moving up a level.

### 4.7 Assessing level [Ch 3, 6, 12]

- **[B]** The chart has 16 levels. Rough bands: beginner 1 to 5, intermediate 6 to 9, advanced 10 to 13, elite 14 to 16.
- **[B]** Level is by ability, not by years trained. Questions: handstand quality, muscle-up, back lever, squat strength.
- **[B]** Typical time: intermediate after about 12 to 24 months of consistent work. Advanced after about 2.5 to 4 years. Heavier athletes (about 175 lb or more) may take longer.
- **[B]** Strong people from weights often have skill and flexibility deficits. These deficits are where progress is.
- **[I]** The charts themselves are images and are not in the conversion. Use exercise names from section 5 to place the athlete.

### 4.8 Beginner vs advanced approaches [Ch 6, 12, 17 to 20]

| Aspect | Beginner | Intermediate | Advanced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Template | Full-body, 3 days | Full-body 3 to 5 days or two-part split | Splits, heavy/light, DUP |
| Focus | Fundamentals, high reps, mobility | Goal-specific work, straight-arm prep | Weak links, tissue care, sleep and detail |
| Progress | Often every session | Every few sessions | Weekly or slower |
| Isolation | Rare | For weak links | For weak links and tissue stress |

- **[B]** Eccentric work is discouraged for beginners, except for pull-ups and dips.
- **[B]** Beginner mistakes: skipping consistency, analysis paralysis, constantly switching routines, adding too many exercises.
- **[B]** Beginners should pick two push goals and two pull goals at most, not everything.
- **[B]** The most frequent predictor of injury is a past injury.
- **[B]** Sample routines are examples only. Do not copy them.

### 4.9 Combining with other sports: relevance to climbers and mountain athletes [Ch 3, 6, 13]

- **[B]** Sport skill comes first. Strength work is a support, not a substitute. Train sport skill when fresh.
- **[B]** Suggested ratio for beginners and intermediates: about 80 percent sport practice and 20 percent strength and conditioning. The book says the same for climbing and parkour.
- **[B]** If the athlete practises the sport five times a week or more, avoid large extra strength blocks. Skill-type work may still fit.
- **[B]** If a coach exists, consult the coach. Err toward less.
- **[B]** Do not copy sport movements with weights. Choose exercises that raise general strength or fix weak links.
- **[B]** Example week for a climber: climbing three or four days with strength after sessions, or a week with upper-body strength on non-climbing days and lower-body strength after climbing days. This is the reverse of the usual upper/lower logic because climbing is upper-body heavy.
- **[B]** Climbing and yoga or mobility pairing is shown as an example. Flexibility work helps limits such as heel hooks.
- **[B]** Hand strength for climbing is handled outside the book. It mentions two or three sessions of hangboard, campus or system board work as an add-on.
- **[B]** Active athletes who add bodyweight work should start with two sessions a week and cut some sport load. Review after two weeks.
- **[B]** Hybrid templates combine barbell and bodyweight work. Substitution, complementing and supplementing are the three methods. Keep structural balance.
- **[B]** Barbell work is judged better for leg strength and size.
- **[I]** For a mountain athlete the likely best fit: two short full-body sessions per week, built on rows, pull-ups, push-ups, dips, L-sit and legs, with handstand skill work. Heavy straight-arm skills are optional.
- **[I]** Alpine and endurance athletes need an aerobic plan from another source. This book adds strength, balance and prehab.

## 5. Movement families

Level numbers below are the book's chart bands. Use them as rough anchors only.

### 5.1 Pulling: pull-ups, rows, levers, muscle-up, cross [Ch 25, 27]

#### Pull-up family

- **Purpose [B]:** vertical pulling, base for climbing-like strength and for one-arm work.
- **Ladder [B]:**
  - Jumping or eccentric pull-ups.
  - Strict pull-ups and chin-ups.
  - L-sit pull-ups.
  - Pull-overs and ring pull-ups.
  - Wide and archer ring pull-ups.
  - One-arm eccentrics, then one-arm chin-up with load.
  - Weighted pull-ups and explosive or clapping variants are side branches.
- **Prerequisites [B/I]:** [I] a controlled hang with shoulders engaged. [B] For one-arm eccentrics: heavy weighted pull-ups, many strict reps, a full supinated back lever, ring tuck planche, and a long one-arm hang.
- **Common errors [B]:** wide elbows, neck craning to get the chin over, partial range, kipping to hide weakness.
- **Joint cautions [B]:** one-arm work stresses the elbow and rotator cuff through torque. Wide grips and behind-the-neck work raise shoulder impingement risk. Kipping with fatigue can load the shoulder badly.
- **Relevance [B/I]:** pull-ups transfer well to climbing strength. [I] Climbers already pull a lot. They usually need more pushing and rear-shoulder work, not more pulling.

#### Row family

- **Purpose [B]:** horizontal pulling for shoulder health and for balance against pressing.
- **Ladder [B]:**
  - Ring-row eccentrics and basic ring rows.
  - Wide and archer rows.
  - Archer with arm in.
  - Straddle one-arm and one-arm rows.
  - Front lever rows (tuck, advanced tuck, straddle, full) as the hard branch.
  - Rope climbing is a grip and pulling branch with limited coverage.
- **Prerequisites [I]:** basic bodyline control, enough strength for a few strict reps of the base row.
- **Common errors [B]:** hips sagging, no pause at the top, shoulders shrugging up.
- **Tip [B]:** hold the top for several seconds when the rear shoulder is weak.
- **Relevance [I]:** rows are the first tool for climbers and desk workers who need posture balance. They also suit limited equipment (a table, a bar, rings).

#### Back lever and front lever

- **Purpose [B]:** straight-arm pulling strength, shoulder and elbow conditioning, base for the cross.
- **Back lever ladder [B]:**
  - German hang and skin the cat.
  - Tuck, advanced tuck, straddle and one-leg-out back lever.
  - Full back lever.
  - Pull-out and bent-arm variations.
  - Handstand lower-down at the top.
- **Front lever ladder [B]:**
  - Tuck, advanced tuck, straddle, one-leg-out and full front lever.
  - Pulls to inverted hang and circle levers at higher levels.
- **Prerequisites [B]:** support holds, German hangs, solid pull-ups and rows. Train the back lever before the front lever.
- **Common errors [B]:** bent arms, arched or piked bodyline, pulling the shoulders up.
- **Joint cautions [B]:**
  - Elbows and biceps tendon take heavy load. Soreness is expected at first. Back off if it persists.
  - Supinated grip is favoured but can be skipped if elbow history makes it unsuitable.
  - Strongly tense the biceps during holds.
  - If elbows hurt: drop the exercise for one or two weeks, use mobility and biceps isolation work, then return a level lower.
  - Excess elbow hyperextension needs extra prehab.
  - Shoulder flexibility limits the German hang. Use foot support when it is too much.
- **Relevance [I]:** high elbow and shoulder demand. The book warns about tendon load. For climbers with elbow history, treat lever work as optional and add it slowly.

#### Muscle-up

- **Purpose [B]:** links the pull-up and dip through a transition. A key beginner-to-intermediate goal.
- **Ladder [B]:**
  - Negatives.
  - Kipping muscle-ups.
  - Strict ring muscle-ups with false grip.
  - Wide or no-false-grip variants and strict bar versions.
  - Combinations with levers and planche at higher levels.
- **Prerequisites [B]:** about five chest-to-bar pull-ups and five full ring dips, plus coaching on the transition.
- **Common errors [B]:** wrong false-grip placement, weak transition rowing, wrist skin tears.
- **Cautions [B]:** the false grip stresses the wrist. If skin tears, train something else until healed.
- **Relevance [I]:** useful for mantling-type strength and for ring skill. Needs wrist and false-grip tolerance.

#### Iron cross and related

- **Purpose [B]:** elite straight-arm ring strength. The book says it deserves its own chapter.
- **Prerequisites [B]:** a long list covering ring handstand push-ups, straight-arm press handstands, full back lever, one-leg-out front lever, ring advanced tuck planche and deep ring dips with turned-out hands.
- **Cautions [B]:** high elbow and shoulder tendon load. Wide-arm work raises impingement risk.
- **Relevance [I]:** not a sensible goal for most mountain athletes. Mention it only as the end of the pulling tree.

#### Cross-links [B]

- Strength levels link across pulling skills. Examples: a straddle front lever matches roughly half-bodyweight pull-ups. A one-arm chin-up matches roughly 80 to 90 percent of bodyweight pull-up strength. Use these as rough bridges, not tests.

### 5.2 Pushing: push-ups, dips, handstand push-ups, planche [Ch 24, 26]

#### Push-up family

- **Purpose [B]:** horizontal pushing, base for planche, and balance for pulling.
- **Ladder [B]:**
  - Standard and diamond push-ups.
  - Ring push-ups, wide, then rings turned out.
  - Archer variants.
  - Pseudo planche push-ups with growing lean.
  - Maltese-style push-ups at the top.
  - One-arm push-up branch (hands-elevated to straight-body).
  - Clapping push-ups are listed as unranked.
- **Common errors [B]:** sagging or arching, limited range, elbows flaring.
- **Cautions [B]:** rings and turned-out hands add wrist and elbow stress. Lean-based work loads the wrist.
- **Relevance [I]:** simplest balancing tool for pull-heavy athletes. Requires little equipment.

#### Dips

- **Purpose [B]:** the "squat of the upper body". Recommended as the first vertical push for beginners.
- **Ladder [B]:**
  - Jumping dips and dip eccentrics.
  - Parallel bar dips.
  - Ring support holds, then ring dips.
  - L-sit and wide ring dips.
  - Turned-out ring dips with growing depth and lean.
  - Weighted dips. One-arm dip is a side branch.
- **Prerequisites [B]:** support hold with straight arms and good shoulder position.
- **Cautions [B]:** deep and wide ring dips load the anterior shoulder, chest and inner elbow. The book uses them as iron-cross preparation.
- **Relevance [I]:** beneficial for climbers' balance. Increase depth slowly if shoulders feel tight in front.

#### Handstand push-ups and presses

- **Purpose [B]:** overhead pressing and shoulder control. The book says a freestanding handstand push-up is about 85 to 95 percent of a bodyweight overhead press.
- **Ladder [B]:**
  - Pike push-ups, box headstand push-ups.
  - Wall headstand push-ups, eccentrics first.
  - Wall handstand push-ups.
  - Freestanding and ring-based versions.
  - Bent-arm and straight-arm press to handstand as a related skill ladder.
- **Prerequisites [B]:** stable wall handstand. Mobility for a fully open overhead shoulder.
- **Common errors [B]:** flared elbows (easier but less useful and riskier), head forward, arching.
- **Cautions [B]:** the book lists handstand push-ups as higher risk for impingement. It does not recommend them early for beginners in the injury chapter. Keep elbows tucked.
- **Relevance [I]:** optional for climbers. Wall handstand holds give overhead shoulder mobility with less risk.

#### Planche

- **Purpose [B]:** straight-arm horizontal pushing strength.
- **Ladder [B]:**
  - Frog stand and straight-arm frog stand.
  - Tuck, advanced tuck, straddle, one-leg-out, full planche.
  - Planche push-ups and pseudo planche push-ups as supplements.
  - Band-assisted work as a bridge.
- **Prerequisites [B]:** push-up and dip base. Wrist conditioning.
- **Common errors [B]:** arched spine and bent arms, which make it easier but reduce training value. Hips too high.
- **Cautions [B]:** wrist and elbow load is high. Hands-to-the-side placement is suggested as the least wrist-irritating option.
- **Relevance [I]:** low for mountain sport. A good strength skill but time-expensive.

### 5.3 Legs [Ch 27, Ch 9, Ch 13]

- **Purpose [B]:** the book says barbells beat bodyweight for leg strength and size. It still teaches bodyweight options.
- **Main ladder [B]:**
  - Mobility check: deep squat position (Asian squat).
  - Parallel and full squats.
  - Side-to-side squats.
  - Pistols (single-leg squats) with assistance, then weighted pistols.
  - Lunge to deep step-up as the other main track. Also chair lunge, stair and chair step-ups, single-leg lunge and shrimp squat.
  - Explosive options: jumps, sprints, hill sprints, cycling.
- **Hamstring and glute work [B]:** only brief mentions. Glute-ham raises, reverse hyperextensions, natural hamstring curls, deadlift variations and hamstring eccentrics appear in routines and examples. The book has no full progression ladder for them.
- **Prerequisites [I]:** ankle, hip and knee mobility to reach depth.
- **Common errors [B]:** knees falling inward, rushing the descent. Control the knee over the toes.
- **Cautions [B]:** lower-back rounding under load; high squat and deadlift volume can irritate the back. Knee pain is linked to poor hip control.
- **Relevance [I]:** high for mountain athletes. Single-leg strength, step-ups and eccentric control relate to descents and carries. Combine with the aerobic plan. Keep leg load away from key endurance sessions.

### 5.4 Core: L-sit, V-sit, manna, hollow, plank, flags, wheel [Ch 25, 27, 9]

- **Purpose [B]:** trunk control for all skills. The L-sit path also trains rear shoulder and active flexibility.
- **L-sit / V-sit / manna ladder [B]:**
  - Tuck L-sit, one-leg-bent L-sit, L-sit, straddle L-sit.
  - Rings-turned-out L-sit.
  - V-sit angles with hands turned back, then manna.
  - Compression work (pulling the knees toward the face while seated).
- **Other core items [B]:**
  - Planks: 25-second, 60-second, one-arm one-leg.
  - Ab-wheel: knees, ramp, eccentrics, full wheel, weighted, one-arm.
  - Hanging leg raises, V-ups, dragon flags, weighted decline sit-ups.
  - Flags and elbow levers in chapter 27.
- **Positions [B]:** hollow, arch, plank and reverse plank are warm-up drills for bodyline.
- **Prerequisites [B]:** hamstring and hip flexibility for V-sit work. Compression is a limiting factor.
- **Common errors [B]:** shoulders drifting forward, rounding the chest, leaning back instead of pushing the hips forward.
- **Cautions [B]:** the book assumes lower-back work comes from weighted leg training. If not, add a bodyweight option. Dragon flags are in routine examples with little technique detail.
- **Relevance [I]:** core work helps body tension on rock. L-sit time is also a low-equipment tool for time-limited athletes.

### 5.5 Skill and static holds [Ch 24, 8, 23]

- **Handstand [B]:**
  - The foundation skill. Practise nearly daily.
  - Ladder: wall handstand (levels 1 to 4), freestanding, then one-arm support variants, ring handstands, one-arm handstand.
  - Goal: stable straight line with locked arms, pushed tall, ribs and glutes tight.
  - Consistency over best result. Fight to hold. Avoid habitual bailing.
- **Static holds [B]:** planche, levers and cross are strength work, not skill work. Place them in the strength block.
- **Support holds [B]:** straight-arm support on bars or rings is a basic tissue-conditioning drill.
- **Isometrics debate [B]:** some athletes can reach holds through movement-based work. Isometrics build strength in a narrow range, so movement-based plans have broader strength. Keep isometrics if a specific hold is the goal.
- **Relevance [I]:** handstand skill is a low-fatigue add-on, good for shoulder mobility.

### 5.6 Rings [Ch 23, 26, 27]

- **Purpose [B]:** rings are the book's core tool. They give unstable loading, make many exercises harder, and carry over to advanced skills.
- **Elements [B]:** ring support and turned-out support, ring rows and push-ups, ring dips, ring statics, muscle-ups, elbow levers, kipping skills (kips and felges), strap handstands.
- **Cautions [B]:**
  - Rings are hard on the elbows.
  - Turned-out support is a key preparation step for straight-arm and dip work.
  - Kips and felges come from the gymnastics code of points and are skill-based. [I] Learn them with a coach.
- **Equipment [B]:** rings are the one required item. Parallettes, a bar and a wall are useful. Home-made options and a broomstick on a tree are suggested.
- **Relevance [I]:** rings give a flexible home setup. They need hang points and care with load.

### 5.7 Other items present

- **Elbow levers and flags [B]:** one-arm balance and strength skills in the multi-plane chapter. Treat as advanced.
- **Rope climbing [B]:** good for grip and pulling strength. The book gives it little space.
- **Kipping muscle-up and kip skills [B]:** skill-based, not main strength builders.
- **Flexibility goals [B]:** splits, bridges, compression and wrist or shoulder range are treated as goals in their own right.

## 6. Agent decision guide

Use this as a starting point. Adjust to the athlete's answers in section 7.

| Untrained beginner | Full-body three days or two, high-rep fundamentals (push-ups, rows, dips or assisted dips, pull-up work, squats, support holds), mobility, wall handstand practice. No advanced statics. | [B] Ch 3, 6, 17 |
| Trained beginner (weights, sport) | Same base but rep range 5 to 8 and focus on skill and flexibility gaps. Handle straight-arm work cautiously because strength outruns tendons. | [B] Ch 6, 18 |

## 7. Questions to ask the athlete before planning

1. What is the main goal and the date, if any? (Climbing grade, alpine trip, race, general strength?)
2. What does the current weekly sport training look like? (Days, hours, intensity, hard days.)
3. What is the training history in bodyweight work, weights and the main sport? How consistent in the last three months?
4. Which exercises can you do now, with how many clean reps? (Pull-ups, push-ups, dips, squats, L-sit, handstand time.)
5. Do you have any current or past injuries in fingers, elbows, shoulders, wrists, back, knees? Any numbness, tingling or clicking that is new?
6. Is there pain during or after any exercise? Which one, and when?
7. Have you seen a clinician for any of this? Is there advice to follow?
8. What equipment and space do you have? (Bar, rings, parallettes, wall, weights, hang points.)
9. How many days and minutes per session can you commit?
10. How is your sleep, stress and nutrition? Any recent illness?
11. How do your pushing and pulling compare? Is posture or desk work an issue?
12. Do you have a coach for the main sport? Does the coach have rules for strength work?
13. Do you want skills (handstand, muscle-up, levers) or general strength?
14. What is your age, body mass and previous training layoff?
15. Do you want to track and log training? Which format?

[I] Do not guess values for missing answers. Ask first, or state clearly the assumption you make.

## 8. Safety

### 8.1 General

- **[B]** The book is educational. It tells readers to see medical professionals before starting and when injured. It does not give diagnoses.
- **[B]** It says a clinician's advice overrides the book.
- **[B]** The disclaimer states the user takes all risk.
- **[B]** Self-treatment without professional advice is not recommended.
- **[I]** Do not diagnose. Describe patterns and refer.
- **[I]** Stop and seek urgent help for chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness. This is not in the book. It comes from this skill's rules.

### 8.2 Elbow cautions

- **[B]** Elbow tendons are the common overuse site in lever, ring, one-arm pull and cross work.
- **[B]** Medial elbow pain links to heavy pulling. Lateral elbow pain links to wrist extension load. Triceps tendon pain links to heavy pushing.
- **[B]** Straight-arm work needs gradual preparation with support holds, German hangs and biceps tension.
- **[B]** Signs to back off: ache, tenderness, stiffness in the morning, pain that fades when warm but returns.
- **[B]** Action: remove the offending exercise, do mobility, add pain-free work, return at reduced volume.

### 8.3 Shoulder cautions

- **[B]** Balance push and pull. Include horizontal pulling and rear-shoulder work.
- **[B]** Higher-risk patterns: flared elbows overhead, wide-grip pulling, behind-the-neck work, internal-rotation pulls, fatigued kipping.
- **[B]** Signs of imbalance: clicking, pain at the front or side, instability feeling, strength gap between push and pull.
- **[B]** Suggested fix pattern: high-rep rotator cuff work, scapular work (Y, T, L, P), more of the weaker direction.
- **[B]** Persistent symptoms: see a professional.

### 8.4 Wrist and forearm cautions

- **[B]** Wrists carry body weight in many exercises. Overuse is common, especially on the little-finger side.
- **[B]** Use neutral wrist positions on rings or parallettes when bent-wrist loading hurts.
- **[B]** Phases: remove aggravators, restore mobility and strength (including extensors), then return to compound work.
- **[B]** Skin tears from false grip: reduce frequency and train something else while healing.

### 8.5 Nerve, back and other flags

- **[B]** Tingling, numbness or radiating arm pain should go to a medical professional. Do not self-experiment with nerve techniques.
- **[B]** Back pain needs individual assessment.
- **[B]** Very heavy soreness with swelling, loss of range and dark urine after a hard return to training is an emergency (possible rhabdomyolysis).
- **[B]** New or growing joint clicking can signal imbalance, especially in the shoulder. Long-standing noise is usually not a concern. Seek assessment if noise comes with pain or worsens.

### 8.6 When to regress or stop

- **[B]** Regress when form breaks, joints ache, or progress stalls with signs of fatigue.
- **[B]** Stop a session if the injured area is aggravated or you reach failure on rehab work.
- **[B]** Avoid training through pain. Remove aggravating exercises instead.
- **[B]** If symptoms do not improve within about two weeks of corrective work (three to five sessions), the book says something else is going on. Seek help.
- **[B]** Avoid strength sessions during fever.

### 8.7 Why individual injuries need professional assessment

- **[B]** Injuries differ by person, tissue and cause. Pain does not always match structure. Many pain-free people have imaging findings.
- **[B]** Shoulder and spine pain can come from distant causes (for example scapular mobility).
- **[B]** The book recommends sports-orientated orthopaedic doctors or physiotherapists and asks the reader to question treatments.
- **[I]** An agent cannot examine the athlete. State this limit plainly. Offer general load management only.

## 9. Gaps and uncertainties

### 9.1 Conversion artefacts

- **Missing charts.** The 16-level progression charts, exercise photos, muscle length-tension graphs and technique illustrations are not in the file. Text refers to them ("shown above", "page 1, column 3").
- **Flattened tables.** Weekly schedule tables and the periodisation diagram appear as loose lines. Their layout is lost.
- **Fragment headings.** Headings such as "Chapter 8", "Chapter 10", "Chapter 102" are conversion fragments, not real chapters. Use the contents list and the named sections.
- **Missing lists.** The "common bodyweight faults" section states its purpose but the fault list itself is not in the text.
- **Page-style headings.** Some exercise sections carry chart labels ("Page 4, Column 1") that mean nothing without the charts.
- **Footnote and link residue.** The contents list holds HTML anchors. External links (websites) are not checked and may be dead.
- **Count mismatch.** The introduction says a concept splits into three aspects but lists two (knowledge base and application).

### 9.2 Conflicting or inconsistent statements

- **Warm-up length.** Chapter 6 gives a base of 10 to 15 minutes (its example uses 10) plus age divided by four. Chapter 8 gives a base of 5 to 10 minutes plus age divided by four. Its sample warm-up is about 15 to 20 minutes.
- **Return volume after tendon injury.** Chapter 15 starts at about 40 percent of previous volume and adds about 20 percent a week. Chapter 21 starts at 40 percent and adds about 10 percent a week.
- **Blood flow and healing.** Chapter 6 says high-repetition work pushes blood into tissue to help healing. Chapter 21, discussing massage, calls the idea of raising blood flow to a tendon false. The two statements sit uneasily together.
- **"80/20" has two meanings.** One is the share of training between strength and endurance (chapter 1). The other is sport practice versus strength work (chapter 13). Keep them separate.
- **Handstand push-ups.** Chapter 4 suggests dips before handstand push-ups for most new athletes. Chapters 12 and 17 list headstand push-up variants at beginner levels. Chapter 21 advises against handstand push-up progressions for beginners.
- **Rest range.** Strength rest is given as 2, 3 or 3 to 5 minutes in different places. Hypertrophy rest ranges from 1 to 4 minutes.
- **Isometric versus concentric strength.** The percentage claims (isometric 100 to 120 percent stronger, eccentric 100 to 150 percent stronger) are loosely worded. Treat them as rough.
- **Level mismatches.** The book says some "advanced" skills are intermediate in gymnastics. Levels are not a general fitness scale.
- **Sample-routine rep notation.** The arrow style (for example 5 to 15) is a progression range, not a fixed prescription.

### 9.3 Evidence and scope limits

- Many rules come from the author's coaching and from gym practice. The book mixes research summaries and anecdotes. It often says "studies show" without a citation in the text. A short reading list is at the end.
- Statements about CNS fatigue, "dual factor" theory and hypertrophy types are simplified or contested. The book itself says the CNS fatigue idea is unclear.
- The injury chapter is general. It contains treatment opinions (RICE versus MEAT, anti-inflammatories, massage, nerve glides) that may be dated or debated. Treat them as background, not advice.
- Nutrition guidance is short and generic.
- The book does not cover: finger strength, tendon pulley injury, hangboard training, grip training for climbing in depth, aerobic base building, altitude, loaded carries, rope or alpine skills.
- Hamstring, glute and calf progression ladders are absent.
- Bodyweight training for women, children and older people is touched on only in short passages.
- Time estimates for reaching levels are typical, not guaranteed.
- No page-level verification was done against the printed book.

Some sub-sections of chapters 2, 10, 11, 13 and 16 were skimmed or read only through summaries.
