# Training for the Uphill Athlete: extended agent summary

## 1. Identity, scope, how to use this file

**Book.** *Training for the Uphill Athlete: A Manual for Mountain Runners and Ski Mountaineers*, by Steve House, Scott Johnston, and Kílian Jornet. Published 2019 by Patagonia (copyright page in the conversion); the foreword is dated May 2018. The authors write in a shared voice. The foreword says Scott Johnston did most of the writing. Kílian Jornet adds "Notes from Kílian" sidebars and a set of race recommendations. About 22 athlete stories appear throughout.

**Scope.** Aerobic-first endurance training for three sports:
- mountain running (VK, 50K, 100K, 100 miles, skyrunning),
- skimo racing,
- ski mountaineering (heavier gear, slower tempo, trips and traverses).

The authors say the method also suits recreational athletes without race numbers.

**Not in scope.** Technical climbing, alpine route skills, detailed nutrition plans, gear reviews, and medical care. The book covers these only in passing.

**Source file.** `../Training for the Uphill Athlete-to-markdown.md` (6,385 lines). Line anchors in section 2 point to chapter starts in that file.

**Tags used here.**
- `[BOOK]` means the book states it. Numbers carry a chapter or section hint.
- `[INFER]` means my inference, an application, or a caution. The book does not state it.
- `[GAP]` means the conversion lacks the content, or the book is silent.

**How to use this file.**
1. Read sections 3 and 4 before you give any zone or intensity advice.
2. Use section 6 to check progression, week structure, and event planning.
3. Use section 8 to choose priorities. Use section 9 before you write any plan.
4. Open the source file for exact wording, worked examples, and the athlete stories. This file contains no copied tables or full weekly plans.
5. Do not treat the book's numbers as a prescription for a specific person. The authors repeat that the book is not a recipe. `[BOOK]`
6. Do not copy elite examples. The Kílian Jornet, Mike Foote, and other pro volumes are context, not targets. `[BOOK]`

## 3. Core concepts

### 3.1 Endurance as a metabolic quality `[BOOK, Ch. 2]`
- Endurance is the highest work rate you can hold until fatigue forces a slowdown. Fatigue shows as shorter, slower strides.
- The authors group limits into: the brain and nervous system (they favor Noakes's "central governor" idea), oxygen delivery (heart stroke volume is the main limit in healthy people), oxygen use in muscle (most trainable), and muscle fiber type and recruitment.
- Their summary line: endurance is mainly the ability to keep a high rate of ATP production, mostly by aerobic means, for a long time.
- For events under about two hours, the limit is the rate of sustainable energy production. Above about two hours, fuel stores become the main limit.

### 3.2 The performance tripod `[BOOK, Ch. 2]`
1. **VO2 max.** Upper limit of aerobic power. Responds fast in the young or untrained. Hard to change in the well trained. Largely genetic. The authors call it the least useful of the three to chase, and they say it does not measure endurance. Elite athletes often hold or lose VO2 max while performance improves.
2. **Movement economy.** The energy cost of covering ground at a given speed. It has a technical part and a metabolic part. Both are trainable.
3. **Lactate threshold (LT).** The highest sustainable work rate. The authors call pace or heart rate at LT the best single proxy for endurance. Several names mean roughly the same thing: anaerobic threshold, maximal lactate steady state, functional threshold, critical pace.

### 3.3 Aerobic capacity vs aerobic utilization `[BOOK, Ch. 2-3]`
- **Capacity** work raises long-term potential. It is mostly low to moderate intensity, often not event-specific, and has slow, lasting effects. The book borrows this model from swim coach Bob Bowman.
- **Utilization** work uses the capacity you already have, for near-term results. It copies event demands and works fast, but the gains are volatile.
- The authors say utilization work erodes capacity, so capacity work must continue in every phase. Capacity should take most of the time.
- Aerobic capacity cannot be too big. Anaerobic capacity and strength can be too big for these sports. `[BOOK]`
- Athletes with a big base can and must do more utilization work. This explains why copying elites hurts amateurs. `[BOOK]`
- Image used: a "vacuum cleaner" or "motor". A larger aerobic system clears pyruvate and lactate better. `[BOOK]`

### 3.4 Energy systems and the pyruvate fork `[BOOK, Ch. 2]`
- Two ATP pathways: anaerobic glycolysis (fast, small yield, carbohydrate only) and aerobic metabolism in mitochondria (slower, much larger yield, uses fat and carbohydrate).
- Pyruvate from glycolysis either enters mitochondria or turns into lactate plus hydrogen ions. Lactate is a fuel, but hydrogen ion build-up slows glycolysis and forces a slowdown.
- Fat oxidation makes no lactate. This is why the authors prefer fat as a fuel.
- Two ways to raise LT: make less lactate (bigger aerobic capacity, more fat use) and remove it faster (the "lactate shuttle" into slow-twitch fibers). The first needs high-volume low-intensity work. The second needs some high-intensity work. The authors say the two need different training and the amount of high intensity is much smaller than most people think.
- Structural adaptations (more capillaries, mitochondria, heart changes) come from low-intensity volume. They take weeks to months and keep building for years. Functional adaptations (enzymes, lactate transporters) come from high intensity. They appear within days. This is why intensity feels rewarding and tempts people to overuse it. `[BOOK]`

### 3.5 Aerobic Deficiency Syndrome (ADS) `[BOOK, Ch. 2, 5]`
- ADS means a weak aerobic base with a strong glycolytic system. The athlete reaches AeT at a very slow pace, often a walk. It comes from too much middle or high intensity.
- The only cure given is a high volume of low to moderate training. The book describes a top skier who dropped his AeT pace sharply over a four-month base. His heart rate at that pace also rose. He did all aerobic work under AeT.
- Warning from the authors: running faster than AeT to protect your ego slows the base from developing.
- Plateau or decline after a short burst of hard training often means the base has eroded. Adding more intensity is the wrong response. `[BOOK]`

### 3.6 Zones, heart rate, and the thresholds `[BOOK, Ch. 3]`
The book uses a five-zone model anchored to two metabolic markers, not to percentages of maximum heart rate.
- **AeT (aerobic threshold, also VT1).** Top of Zone 2. Breathing is controlled, you can talk and may breathe through the nose. Blood lactate is roughly 2 mmol/L, about 1 mmol/L above baseline. Sustainable for hours.
- **LT (lactate threshold, also VT2).** Top of Zone 3. Deeper, faster breathing; talk is only a few words.
- Zone roles:
  - **Recovery** (below Z1): very easy; helps recovery; heart rate at least about 20 percent below the top of Z1.
  - **Z1 aerobic conditioning:** top is about 10 percent below AeT; lactate below about 1.5 mmol/L; at least 30 minutes continuous; forms the base.
  - **Z2 aerobic capacity:** up to AeT; the strongest driver of AeT; usually 30-120 minutes continuous.
  - **Z3 endurance (tempo):** between AeT and LT; glycolysis dominates; strong muscular-endurance effect; seductive and easy to overuse.
  - **Z4 aerobic power (VO2max-type work):** above LT; about 90-95 percent of max heart rate; interval style.
  - **Z5 maximal anaerobic:** under roughly 30-45 seconds; heart rate lags and is not a useful guide.
- The authors admit all zone systems are flawed. Daily state moves the markers by several beats. They warn that 6-7 zone systems claim false precision. `[BOOK]`
- Heart rate is the main intensity tool for uphill athletes because terrain and conditions vary too much for pace or power. `[BOOK]`
- Zone boundary percentages for Z3-Z5 come from a chart that is not in the conversion. `[GAP]`

### 3.7 The Ten Percent Test (AeT-to-LT spread) `[BOOK, Ch. 3]`
- Divide LT heart rate by AeT heart rate. If the result is within about 10 percent (elite spread 6-7 percent, roughly 10 beats), the base is sufficient to add Z3 work. A bigger spread means aerobic deficiency remains.
- The book's example: AeT 128 and LT 150 gives about 17 percent. Another example in Ch. 5 shows a 40-beat spread (ADS) closing after a season of base work.
- Rules attached to this test:
  - Spread above 10 percent: delay Z3, keep high intensity to at most once a week, do almost all aerobic work in Z2 (Ch. 11).
  - Spread 10 percent or less: up to two weekly high-intensity aerobic sessions are allowed in base; shift routine aerobic work to Z1 and cut Z2.
- The test is the book's main gate for adding intensity. `[BOOK]`

### 3.8 Fat adaptation and fueling physiology `[BOOK, Ch. 2]`
- Fat stores are effectively unlimited; carbohydrate stores are under about 2,000 kcal even in trained athletes. `[BOOK]`
- The authors cite newer studies showing trained athletes burn fat at much higher intensities than older textbooks claimed.
- Ways to build fat use: long low-intensity sessions, some aerobic sessions done fasted (morning, before eating), and diet shifts toward less carbohydrate. They say diet changes matter less above about 12 training hours a week, because volume already drives the effect.
- Benefits claimed: slower glycogen drain and less lactate at submaximal pace, so lower effort for the same speed. `[BOOK]`
- `[INFER]` Fasted training and low-carbohydrate approaches carry risks (poor session quality, under-fueling, illness). Apply them only with a sound recovery and fueling baseline, and refer medical questions to a clinician.

### 3.9 Economy `[BOOK, Ch. 2, 6]`
- Technical economy comes from many hours of event-like movement. Flat road running builds a "movement library" that fails on rough ground, downhills, or skis.
- Strength helps economy by allowing longer strides at a lower fraction of maximum. Falling stride length late in ultras predicts results.
- The glossary notes that economy on flat ground does not transfer directly to steep uphill. `[BOOK]`

### 3.10 Durability `[BOOK]`
- Mike Foote's athlete story argues that in attrition-type 100-milers, finishing order often depends on who slows least and breaks least. He credits volume, strength work, and self-care.
- Kílian's notes tie fast recovery and tolerance of high volume to decades of capacity-building work.
- `[INFER]` The book gives no durability test. Treat durability as the result of progressive volume, specific strength and muscular endurance, downhill exposure, and recovery habits. The authors name no single metric.

### 3.11 Other principles `[BOOK, Ch. 3]`
- **Training effect.** The workout is the stimulus; the adaptation happens in recovery. Too frequent or too sparse stimuli both stall progress.
- **Continuity, gradualness, modulation.** Regular work, small progressive steps, and alternating hard and easy.
- **Specificity.** Better athletes need more event-like training. Beginners gain from any endurance activity. Bikes and swimming are too easy on the body for uphill athletes, so use them for recovery or rehab. Below about six hours or 40 km of running a week, cross-training adds value.
- **Individuality.** Training history, recent training, life stress, and fiber type change responses. The book lists simple clues for fast-twitch vs slow-twitch dominance and matching needs (fast-twitch: more base, short reps; slow-twitch: frequent small doses of speed).
- **Muscular fatigue model.** The "frontier fiber" idea: at any speed the last, highest-power fibers recruited are the first to tire, so they limit pace. Zone 3-4 work and ME work train them.

## 4. Testing and measurement

### 4.1 Aerobic threshold (AeT) tests `[BOOK, Ch. 5, in order of ease]`
1. **MAF formula (Maffetone).** 180 minus age, then adjust: minus 5 for injury, long layoff, or frequent illness; plus 5 for over two years of steady progress. The authors find it conservative and fixed (it cannot follow fitness gains).
2. **Ventilatory cues.** Heart rate you can hold while breathing only through the nose, or talking, correlates well with a 2 mmol/L reading in moderately to well-trained athletes, from the authors' field experience. It fails in people with no aerobic history, a high-intensity bias, or no regular exercise. For those people, use MAF or a lactate test.
3. **Lactate step test.** Treadmill at 5 percent grade, or a moderate hill. 15-minute warm-up. Three-minute stages. Step up about 10 bpm per stage, starting very low. Sample blood at each stage end. AeT is the heart rate at about 2 mmol/L, or at the first stage that is 1 mmol/L above the previous. Hold heart rate steady within each stage.
4. **Lab gas-exchange test.** Gold standard; the book quotes roughly $150-350 (2018). Ask for AeT and LT heart rates and the fat-versus-carbohydrate graph. Standard VO2max protocols use short stages that rush through the aerobic range. Stages need at least three minutes for a valid AeT. Many labs do not know the AeT concept.

### 4.2 Lactate threshold test `[BOOK, Ch. 5]`
- A maximal sustained uphill effort of 30-60 minutes (30 for the less fit). The treadmill is second choice.
- 15-20 minute warm-up, finishing with 2-3 minutes in Z3. Rest the legs for a few days before. Settle on a pace that is maximal but sustainable.
- Average heart rate over the test is LT (top of Z3). A rise of 2-3 bpm usually forces a slowdown soon.

### 4.3 Field gauges and re-checks `[BOOK]`
- Basic checks for Z1-Z2 pace: you can repeat the session day after day; you can talk; you can breathe through the nose; lactate stays under 2 mmol/L; AeT pace improves over weeks.
- Every ~2 weeks: note your time at the same heart rate on a regular course. Falling time means a stronger aerobic system. The book calls this a self-run AeT time trial.
- Benchmark workouts matter more in mountains because terrain varies and times are hard to compare. `[BOOK, Ch. 4]`
- Count total time, including descents, on hilly aerobic days. Do not push on downhills to keep heart rate up. `[BOOK, Ch. 5]`

### 4.4 Limits and cautions
- Zone boundaries move day to day with fatigue. `[BOOK]`
- Heart rate lags at very short, very hard efforts (Z5). `[BOOK]`
- Conversation and nose breathing are rough cues. The authors say they correlate poorly in people with little aerobic training. `[BOOK]`
- `[GAP]` **Heart-rate drift** (cardiac drift, decoupling) tests do not appear in this book. A text search finds no use of "drift". Do not attribute any drift-based test to this source.
- `[GAP]` **Heart rate variability** appears only as one possible overtraining sign (reduced HRV, requiring special equipment). The book gives no HRV protocol.
- `[INFER]` If the athlete has a wrist heart-rate sensor, ask about sensor type. The book assumes a chest-strap style monitor and describes the zones as approximate.
- `[INFER]` Treat any single test as a snapshot. Re-test after a few weeks of consistent training or after a layoff.

### 4.5 Monitoring recovery `[BOOK, Ch. 4]`
- **Perception.** The warm-up doubles as a readiness check. Be ready to shorten or cancel the session. Lack of motivation is a warning sign.
- **Resting heart rate.** Morning, same conditions. A raised value points to load, illness, poor sleep, or stress. The authors call results "spotty" and weigh it with other signs.
- **Step test.** One or two minutes of stepping, enough to raise heart rate by about 50-70 bpm. Note the peak and the value after one minute of sitting. Repeat the same protocol and track the drop over time.
- **Stair test.** Walk or bound stairs you climb daily and note leg feel.
- **Workout grades A to F.** A: strong with reserve; B: good; C: flat or off; D: could not finish or had to cut; F: could not train. Stop and assess after more than two Cs in a row, or a C and a D in one week.
- **Fatigue matrix.** Group A (muscular): sore muscles (peaks around 48 hours; do light aerobic work, swim, rest) versus dead, heavy legs (usually from raising load too fast; very light days, sleep, food). Group B (energy): low drive, waking tired, flat mood (more sleep and food quality, social time, check blood tests, avoid hard sessions).
- **Ferritin.** The book states athletes can underperform below 50 ng/mL, and below 30 is worse. Standard "low" ranges are lower. Suggest a blood test when fatigue persists. `[BOOK]` `[INFER]` Refer the interpretation to a clinician.
- **Overtraining signs.** Higher heart rate at submaximal effort early on, lower later; persistent deep fatigue; mood changes; sleep loss; weight loss; loss of menstrual cycle; blood markers (cortisol, testosterone). The book calls overtraining a medical issue and says it needs weeks of rest.

### 4.6 Training log metrics `[BOOK, Ch. 4, 13]`
- Time is the simplest and best default load metric for mountain athletes. Add vertical gain and loss, because vertical drives muscle load and intensity.
- Kílian logs time, vertical, and distance (running), and for skiing only time and vertical. He also rates technicality on a four-step scale and writes a short feel note. `[BOOK]`
- Example from the book: a week with less distance and more vertical can be harder than a longer flatter week.
- The authors use TrainingPeaks for fitness-and-fatigue tracking. Strava and similar tools log but track less. `[BOOK]`
- `[INFER]` Keep a log for at least one full cycle before using it to plan. The book says the first full log (8+ months) makes planning much better.

## 5. Training types

### 5.1 Aerobic base building (Z1-Z2) `[BOOK, Ch. 5, 9, 11]`
- **Purpose.** Raise AeT, build capillaries and mitochondria, improve fat use, and speed recovery from hard work.
- **When.** All year; main focus in the base period and the first priority for most readers.
- **Dosage logic.**
  - 80-90 percent of annual training volume at or below AeT (Ch. 5). World-class athletes spend about 80-85 percent in Z1-Z2 (Ch. 3).
  - Z1 should be about 50-70 percent of annual aerobic volume (Ch. 3).
  - Z3 about 10 percent or less of annual volume (Ch. 3). Z4 under 10 percent (Ch. 9).
  - Seiler's data on elites (cited): about 80 percent below AeT, 6-8 percent between thresholds, 12-14 percent above LT. The glossary shortens this to 80/20 polarized.
  - Kílian's 2016 split (sidebar, Ch. 3): about 1,300 hours; about 54 percent Z1-Z2, 26 percent Z3, 20 percent racing-type work. He had a heavy race calendar that year.
  - Ch. 5 example of a national-level amateur with a job and family: about 650 hours a year, 12.5 hours a week average. Kílian at about 1,300 hours is the "iceberg" example.
- **Z2 rule.** With ADS, do nearly all aerobic work in Z2 (it is slow enough not to hurt). With a narrow spread, Z2 is too taxing: limit it to one or two days and about 10-15 percent of weekly volume, do the rest in Z1 and recovery.
- **Volume check.** If you cannot repeat the same Z1-Z2 workout tomorrow and the day after, volume or intensity is too high.
- **Session shape.** Continuous, at least 30 minutes, up to several hours. A weekly long day takes about 30-40 percent of the week (Ch. 11 B week).
- **Pick-ups.** 8-10 seconds early, up to 15 seconds later; 6-8 per session at most; at least 2 minutes easy between; once a week; start after a few weeks of base. Ch. 9 lists a wider range (6 x 15 sec up to 8 x 30 sec in a 45-75 minute session). The book is not fully consistent here. Never sprint.
- **Higher-intensity continuous work.**
  - Tempo (Z3): 20-60 minutes (Ch. 5; Ch. 9 gives 20-40). Steady effort, not a time trial. Narrow heart-rate range when fit.
  - Progressive distance: start easy, rise through Z2 and Z3, finish briefly in Z4 if feeling strong; best beyond 90 minutes.
  - One progressive run per 10-14 days after early base.
- **Prerequisites.** None for beginners except patience. The book says start easy and build.
- `[INFER]` The book's main risk list for aerobic volume is impact and overuse, so build running volume slowly (see 6.6).

### 5.2 Muscular endurance (ME) `[BOOK, Ch. 5, 8]`
- **Purpose.** Train the high-power fibers that limit pace, so legs do not fail before breathing. This is the book's most effective strength form for these sports.
- **Signs you need it.** You slow in sessions under two hours without running low on fuel; legs go heavy or dead; downhills wreck you; legs burn during intervals while heart rate sags.
- **Method.** Add resistance so legs, not lungs, limit the work: steep ground (20-50 percent grade), long bounding strides, or extra weight (10-15 percent of body weight in a pack to start).
- **When.** Base period, as preparation before Z3 and Z4 intervals. Drop or cut it when Z3 and Z4 start (Z3/Z4 work keeps the effect). Ski mountaineers keep it weekly because their pace is slow and loaded.
- **Dosage.**
  - Frequency: once a week for beginners; up to twice for experienced athletes. Space ME and Z3/Z4 sessions by 72 hours. Keep high Z1 volume alongside.
  - Gym ME (Ch. 8): four leg exercises (box step, lunge, split jump squat, squat jump), six sets of ten per leg, about one rep a second, short rests; start bodyweight; add resistance in steps over about eight to twelve sessions (10 percent body weight, later 15-20 percent). Sessions can be spaced up to ten days apart; repeat the previous step if the gap is over twelve days.
  - Outdoor ME: 8-10 minutes uphill at a low leg burn, twice, building toward a 40-minute total; or bounding repeats of 10-20 seconds with recovery.
  - Runners with a good base (Ch. 13): begin after at least four weeks of Z1-Z2 only, one per week for about eight weeks.
- **Warning.** Legs can feel it for days. If hill climbing does not improve week to week, the authors say you are likely doing too much. `[BOOK]`
- **Upper-body ME for skimo.** Single-pole work on roller skis (flat start, then short hard reps, then 10-minute reps) or on a ski ergometer (an eight-week progression of more reps and shorter rests). Can be added twice a week in early base as a second session. The book estimates the upper body at about 5-10 percent of skinning propulsion.

### 5.3 General strength `[BOOK, Ch. 6-7]`
- See section 7.1.

### 5.4 Power: hill sprints and bounding (Z5) `[BOOK, Ch. 5, 8, 9]`
- **Purpose.** Recruit fast-twitch fibers, improve specific leg strength, stride length, and economy; also neural learning. Not aimed at lactate tolerance.
- **When.** From early base, once basic strength is adequate. About once a week in base and intensity weeks. Skiers can add poles.
- **Dosage logic.**
  - Reps of 8-10 seconds (Ch. 5); Ch. 9 gives 10-30 seconds. Use 10 seconds as the safer reading, and do not extend beyond it in progression (Ch. 5).
  - Hill grade about 30 percent; steeper is better until grip fails; about 50 percent is a practical ceiling.
  - Start with 6 reps; add no more than 2 reps a week (Ch. 5, 9). Rest 1-2 minutes minimum, with 2 minutes walking down; 3 minutes between sets over 8 reps.
  - New athletes begin at 80-95 percent effort for the first two or three sessions.
  - Warm-up is long and specific (about 20 minutes jogging, dynamic drills, short build-ups). Injury risk is real.
- Feeling too easy is normal early; the authors say that means you need it. If power falls and longer rests do not help, stop the session.
- Taper and recovery weeks cut hill-sprint volume (half in recovery; about 30 percent weekly in taper).

### 5.5 Intervals and VO2max-type work (Z3, Z4) `[BOOK, Ch. 3, 5, 9, 11]`
- **Purpose.** Raise LT speed and its duration (Z3); max out aerobic and glycolytic systems and lactate removal (Z4).
- **Prerequisites.** Sufficient base (Ten Percent Test), enough muscular endurance, rested legs.
- **Z3 threshold intervals.** 20-60 minutes of total work. Start with 3-6 minute reps and work to rest of 3:1 or 4:1. Experienced athletes may reach 3 x 20 or 2 x 30. Ch. 9 lists 8-15 minute reps, 2-4 minutes recovery, 40-60 minutes total.
- **Z4 aerobic power.** 15-30 minutes total work. Reps 30 seconds to 8 minutes. Rest about 1:1 up to 2 minutes, active. Same pace and heart rate each rep. Stop when legs force heart rate below Z4.
- **30/30s.** The book's preferred Z4 entry. Reps of 30 seconds hard and 30 seconds easier, building to Z4 by the second rep; progress sets from 2 x 6 minutes toward 20-30 minutes total.
- **Another Kílian favorite.** Two minutes high tempo, two minutes active rest, two minutes long strides; repeat until pace and heart rate fade.
- **Seiler study (cited).** Well-trained athletes did best with 4 x 8 minutes (2 minutes rest) compared with 4 x 4 and 4 x 16. Less-trained athletes may not hold those reps. The book suggests Z3 and ME work first for most people.
- **Introduction rules (Ch. 11).**
  - Start Z3 at about 5 percent of weekly time, then at most about +10 percent per week toward about 10 percent. Recover in 48 hours; if legs are tired at 72, cut back.
  - Add Z4 only after Z3 is comfortable at about 10 percent. Replace Z3 with half as much Z4 (two minutes of Z3 becomes one of Z4). Begin Z4 at no more than about 5 percent of weekly volume.
  - Keep Z3 and Z4 on separate days and 72 hours apart.
  - Drop Z2 when adding Z3/Z4. Do not let intensity cut Z1-Z2 volume by more than about 5 percent.
  - Start adding Z3 within about two months of the first race even if the base is imperfect.
  - Kílian's preferred way to add intensity is Z3.
- **Intensity mix.** Events under two hours need more Z3-Z5; events over three hours are mostly aerobic. Fitter athletes handle longer reps (over six minutes). `[BOOK]`
- **Anaerobic endurance.** Rare; two options (2-minute max reps at 2:1 work to rest, 4-6 times; or 20-30 seconds with 2-3 minutes rest, 8-12 reps). Two or three sessions spaced 7-10 days apart may be enough; mainly for VK and short skimo events. Kílian does two or three short-interval sessions before the season.

### 5.6 Hill-specific and vertical work `[BOOK, Ch. 11-13]`
- Over 80 percent of skimo time and over 50 percent of running time is uphill, so uphill speed gets most attention.
- Skimo and ski mountaineering base volume is counted in vertical meters, with time on foot or skis as the main work.
- Weekly vertical rules (Ch. 11, 13):
  - Beginners: start with no more than one third to one half of the goal event's vertical in week one (Ch. 11) or half (Ch. 13).
  - Reach at least the full event vertical in two late-base or precompetition weeks, matching big distance weeks.
  - Cut vertical 40-60 percent in recovery weeks.
  - Increase vertical by no more than about 10 percent week to week, and about 20 percent across a four-week block.
- Choose goals the available terrain can support, and train on slopes like the race. `[BOOK]`

### 5.7 Downhill and eccentric conditioning `[BOOK, partial]`
- Downhill running skill needs practice. Short fast downhill reps (1-2 minutes) inside easy aerobic runs, with more reps over weeks. `[BOOK, Ch. 5]`
- Base and recovery weeks add a few quick downhill pick-ups, usually in two runs a week (Ch. 11).
- Downhills have a strengthening effect; count them in volume (Ch. 5). Hard downhills cause soreness that peaks around 48 hours (Ch. 4).
- ME work is credited with resisting late-race muscle breakdown from downhill pounding (Ch. 6).
- John Kelly's story notes that treadmill-heavy training cut downhill and "trail muscle" work.
- `[GAP]` The book has no dedicated eccentric-loading or downhill-strength program. `[INFER]` Treat the points above as the full book coverage. Advice on eccentric blocks (for example, tendon or quadriceps preparation) would come from outside this source.

### 5.8 Running economy and technique `[BOOK]`
- The book says technique is learned by repetition and coaching, not from a book (Ch. 2). It lists a movement library problem for flat-trained runners.
- Economy levers it names: strength, hill sprints, fast flatter running when the athlete is strong uphill but slow on faster terrain (Ch. 11), terrain-matched training, and fatigue resistance through ME.
- Kílian says he studied running mechanics and relaxation. `[BOOK]`
- `[GAP]` No drills or form cues are given.

### 5.9 Skills (skimo) `[BOOK, Ch. 12]`
- Technique list: transitions, kick turns, skin management, boot packing, high-tempo skinning, fast skiing downhill.
- Learn slowly and with control first. Then practice a 10-15 minute circuit at about Z3 effort, adding laps as skill and fitness grow.
- Ski simulations on dry land: ski striding (long, low-intensity), moose hoofing (loping stride at Z3-like effort), ski bounding (explosive, in seconds). Roller skis are optional and carry downhill risk.
- Ski mountaineers swap Z3/Z4 for ME because of heavy gear and slow tempo. `[BOOK]`

### 5.10 Recovery sessions and warm-up `[BOOK, Ch. 3, 5]`
- Recovery workouts: very easy, 30-60 minutes for most, may be a walk or swim. They should leave you better than you started. They take a growing share of volume as AeT pace rises.
- Warm-up: for Z1-Z2, ease in over about 10 minutes. For intense sessions, a longer routine with light aerobic work, dynamic drills, and 2-3 minutes in Z3-Z4.
- Cool-down: 20-30 minutes in recovery or Z1 after hard work.
- Recovery methods ranked by the authors: diet, sleep, massage and self-massage, lower life stress, recovery workouts.
- Post-exercise food: roughly 100-200 kcal within about 30 minutes after sessions over an hour, carbohydrate to protein about 3:1 or 4:1. Eating during and after long sessions shortens recovery.

## 6. Planning

### 6.1 Principles `[BOOK, Ch. 3, 9]`
- Plans are guidance. Writing the plan is easy; carrying it out and adapting is the hard part.
- Lay out workouts only one to two weeks ahead (Ch. 11), inside a macro plan.
- A moderate plan well executed beats a perfect plan done badly.
- Set a goal and keep it realistic. Compute available hours (subtract work, sleep, travel, family) as an upper bound. `[BOOK]`

### 6.2 Season structure and periodization `[BOOK, Ch. 4, 9-11]`
- Terms: macrocycle (season or year), mesocycles (weeks to months, one target each), microcycles (usually 7 days).
- Periods:
  1. **Transition.** General conditioning; 2-8 weeks.
  2. **Base.** The main period. Capacity first; expect mild fatigue and weaker race results. Only C-level events here.
  3. **Precompetition.** Event-specific and utilization work; measured in weeks.
  4. **Competition.** Spend capacity; plan rebuilding blocks between races.
- Capacity is cheaper to maintain than to build. `[BOOK]`
- Some experienced athletes keep all qualities year-round. The authors say this needs a deep history and self-knowledge. `[BOOK]`

### 6.3 Planning to an event date `[BOOK, Ch. 9-13]`
1. Write events on a calendar and label them A, B, or C.
   - A: build the plan to it; real taper; may need long recovery; allow weeks of base between two A races.
   - B: tune-up or test; short drop in load before and after.
   - C: train through it.
2. Choose Category 1 or 2 (see 6.5).
3. Pick a macrocycle plan of the right shape from Ch. 11 figures and fill each week with a letter. `[GAP]` The figures are missing from the conversion.
4. Pick workouts from the week-type menu (6.4) and place them one to two weeks ahead.
5. Minimum lead: the book's first-50K plan assumes at least 20 weeks, and 6+ weeks of transition if recent running is thin. Luke Nelson suggests planning 6-12 months ahead for popular races. `[BOOK]`
6. If an athlete only wants fitness, pure base training is a legitimate plan. `[BOOK]`

### 6.4 Weekly structure: the six week types `[BOOK, Ch. 11]` (own words)
- **B (base).** Accumulate Z1 volume (Z2 sparingly or mostly Z2 with ADS). Two strength sessions, two core sessions, recovery workouts, a long day of about 30-40 percent of weekly volume, at least four other aerobic sessions, two with fast-downhill pick-ups. ME if it is a weakness.
- **I (intensity).** Same base plus Z3, then Z4. One hill-sprint session, two core sessions. Drop ME (ski mountaineers still do ME, up to twice).
- **R (recovery).** About 50 percent of volume (the progression rules say 40-60). One core session, half the sprint volume, at least two recovery workouts, a long day of about 40 percent of the lower volume, late in the week; half the intensity. Loosely structured; you should want to attack next week by the end.
- **S (specificity).** Ultras and ski mountaineering: back-to-back long days making up 60-80 percent of weekly volume; one Z4 session at least 72 hours from them; no Z3. VK and short skimo: cut aerobic base, add a second Z4 session 72 hours after the first; weekly Z4 time no more than 15 percent above the prior week.
- **T (taper).** Long day about 50 percent of volume; short sharp intensity cut about 30 percent each week; one core session; hill sprints cut 30 percent weekly. Do not sneak in new work.
- **G (goal week).** No strength; recovery-zone sessions only (at most four days); two sessions with three or four 30-second accelerations and 3 minutes easy between (none for ski mountaineers).
- Active recovery (stretch, roll, massage, swim) at least three times a week in every week type.

### 6.5 Level and categories `[BOOK, Ch. 10-13]`
- **Category 1 (build a base).** Any of: 400 hours or fewer a year averaged over two years; under 18; no successful event at the target distance in the last 12 months. Priority is more volume, not more intensity. Expect repeated base cycles if well under 400 hours.
- **Category 2 (maintain and add).** All of: over 400 hours a year for two years; over 18; successful event at or near target distance in the past 12 months; races several times a year. Priority is specificity and some more intensity, without cutting Z1-Z2 much.
- **Totally new to structure (Ch. 10).** Conservative annual hour targets by age, from 300 (under 16 or over 50) through 350 (35-50), 400 (20-35) up to 450 (16-19). Build slowly.
- **Novice:** 8-week transition, then a long base with weekly vertical growth, first 50K or first skimo races or a hut tour. `[BOOK]`
- **Intermediate:** repeat base cycles, add Z3 within about two months of racing, use B races as tests. `[BOOK]`
- **Advanced:** specificity, multiple races, rebuild blocks, tapering by priority. `[BOOK]`
- Running volume guides (Ch. 13), minimum peak weeks and context:
  - 50K: comfortable 40 km weeks before the plan; peak about 80-88 km; average about 50 km over the biggest 12 weeks.
  - 100K: comfortable at 50K loads; peak about 120-130 km; average about 75 km over 16 weeks.
  - 100 miles: comfortable at 100K loads; peak about 145-160 km; average about 100 km over 18 weeks.
  - Moving up in distance: raise volume first; add intensity later.
  - The first 100 miles (about 62 km) of running only conditions the legs.
- Skimo progression figures give weekly vertical targets (for example, an average of 1,500-1,700 m a week over the previous eight weeks for a first-season athlete). The tables are missing, so use the numbers only as the book's quoted context. `[GAP]`

### 6.6 Volume progression and recovery weeks `[BOOK, Ch. 3, 10, 11]`
- Annual volume: beginners (under 350-400 hours) may grow up to about 25 percent a year; advanced (over 500 hours) cap at about 10 percent. Kílian recommends 10 percent a year. Marc Pinsach's story agrees.
- Transition: no more than 7-10 percent a week unless you have a multi-year systematic history. Return to running over 4-6 weeks after ski season.
- Base: at most 10 percent week to week for distance, vertical, and time, except after a recovery week. At most three build weeks in a row. Do not raise distance, vertical, and intensity in one week; if two rise, keep the total under 15 percent. Avoid two weeks in a row above 15 percent.
- Modulation: a 3:1 build-to-recovery cycle is the default. Use 2:1 or 1:1 for those with little experience of intensity. After planned overreaching, drop load by as much as 70 percent.
- Recovery week: 40-60 percent of the last big week, at most one intensity session.
- Overreaching (short planned overload) is for advanced athletes with low life stress. `[BOOK]`
- `[INFER]` Dates are the default; a "week" shifts when life disrupts it. The authors expect deviation.

### 6.7 Adapting to missed training `[BOOK, Ch. 3-4]`
- Breaks of a day or two are fine if total monthly volume drops by no more than about 5 percent. Larger or frequent gaps need a step back in the plan; do not pretend the work was done.
- After a fatigue-related break over two days: restart with easy sessions at about 50 percent of normal duration for a couple of days, then about 75 percent, checking each time.
- Illness: stop training at the first signs. Heavy training can suppress immunity, especially in the first hour after hard sessions. A minor cold takes two to three days to clear. Return with one easy day for each day lost, starting in Z1-Z2. The book rejects "training through it."
- Planned breaks (travel): build a heavier week before and treat the break as recovery. Plan weeks ahead.
- Transition after a break over four weeks: begin at about 50 percent of the prior average weekly hours. After a sport switch with under two weeks off: about 80 percent.
- `[INFER]` Use the log, not memory, to decide where to resume.

### 6.8 Tapering `[BOOK, Ch. 4, 11]`
- Taper is individual. Kílian often skips it when he races a lot.
- A races get the most taper; B less; C none.
- The T week and G week rules are in 6.4. Keep intensity short and rests long. Race week activity should keep you loose, not tired.
- A race is a utilization effort. Between races: two weeks minimum for shorter races and four to six weeks for ultras if you want a rebuilding block. With three weeks between races, there may be only about ten days of capacity work after recovery and taper. `[BOOK, Ch. 4]`
- Race-season scenarios in Ch. 12: a three-week gap for capacity rebuilding; back-to-back A weekends (maintenance only); a B or C race feeding an A race (race is the hard workout; light days around it).
- `[GAP]` No taper length by distance (for example, 100 miles versus VK) is given beyond the week-type rules.

### 6.9 Injury and return `[BOOK, partial]`
- Overuse injuries come mostly from raising running volume too fast. Tendons and fascia adapt slowly. Skiers are prone to running injury.
- Overuse signs and tendon problems are early warnings that adaptation is failing (Ch. 4). The book says no plan works once you are injured.
- Illness and fatigue return rules are in 6.7. Cross-training (bike, swim) can replace running for rehab and recovery.
- Knee-tracking self-tests lead to hip strength work; if no improvement in three weeks, see a physiotherapist (Ch. 7).
- `[GAP]` The book does not cover diagnosis, injury-specific rehab protocols, or return-to-run progressions after structural injury. `[INFER]` Route these to a qualified clinician.

## 7. Strength, injury prevention, fueling, mental, gear and pacing

### 7.1 Strength for mountain athletes `[BOOK, Ch. 6-8]`
- Purpose: performance and injury prevention, not gym numbers. Endurance work uses about a quarter of max strength, thousands of times.
- Strength is mostly neural, endurance is mostly metabolic. Too much mass hurts. Muscle must be carried and fueled.
- General strength: single-leg emphasis; hip (gluteal) stability; core; pull and push for upper body. A rough guide: if you cannot half-squat your own weight you may gain durability, but twice body weight adds little. A skimo racer who cannot do one pull-up likely gains from learning.
- Specific strength: hill sprints and bounding, then ME.
- Assessments (self-tests) and the stage they lead to:
  - Air squat, box step, sit-to-stand for knee tracking (inward collapse over about an inch means Stage 2 or 3 gets chosen as the test gets harder).
  - Push-up and pull-up or chin-up tests for the upper body.
  - Plank and windshield wiper for the core.
- Stage 1: high reps, light resistance, band squats and lateral band work, inclined push-ups, assisted pull-ups, easy core holds. Stage 2: single-leg step-ups, step-downs, lunges at bodyweight to about 30 reps per leg; knee push-ups; isometric pull-up holds. Stage 3: weighted versions at about 6 reps; weighted push-ups; slow-lowering pull-ups; loaded core work.
- Timing: general strength and core twice a week in transition; ongoing support in base (two sessions in B weeks); reduce in recovery, taper, and race weeks.
- `[GAP]` Ch. 9 mentions a "max strength" program (twice a week in early base) and the glossary gives loads of 85-90 percent of one-rep maximum. The chapters in the conversion contain no max-strength program. Do not invent one from this book.
- Skimo upper body: ski-specific poling ME (5.2).
- The authors tell athletes to see a professional coach or physiotherapist when the self-tests do not respond. `[BOOK]`

### 7.2 Nutrition, fueling, hydration `[BOOK, light]`
- Present: post-exercise recovery eating (5.10), fat-adaptation methods (3.8), energy demands that can exceed about 6,000 kcal a day in very heavy training (Ch. 4), a note that diet quality and carbohydrate intake help recovery (Ch. 4 fatigue lists), and a mention that a bad fueling plan sank one athlete's Barkley attempt (story).
- Mike Foote's record story: about 400 kcal an hour was possible for him in 24 hours of skiing. It is an anecdote, not guidance.
- `[GAP]` No race-day fueling plan, carbohydrate-per-hour guidance, sodium, caffeine, or hydration protocol. Hydration is not taught. `[INFER]` Use other sources for these and ask the athlete about gut tolerance in long events.

### 7.3 Mental `[BOOK, scattered]`
- Central governor idea: repeated exposure to discomfort lets the brain allow more strain.
- The authors warn about compulsion to train versus real motivation. Lack of drive can be a fatigue signal.
- Kílian's rules: account for life stress, do not overdo it, keep goals realistic, progress distances step by step, prepare for course technicality, train all linked elements (body, skill, mind, gear), and do not take it too seriously.
- Athlete stories mention problem-solving rather than stacking small complaints in ultras, reframing adversity, "knowing your why," and that low patches pass. These are anecdotes. `[BOOK]`
- Do not copy another athlete's training. Marc Pinsach's story shows a case of overtraining from following Kílian. `[BOOK]`

### 7.4 Gear and pacing `[BOOK, light]`
- Gear: ski mountaineering weight changes tempo and raises ME demand; skimo needs transition skill; the book suggests checking gear, feeding, and tactics at B races. No gear lists.
- Pacing: Z1-Z2 sessions are controlled by heart rate and breathing, not pace; slow down on steep uphills and walk when needed. Mike Foote's record plan used event pace in vertical per hour (his story gives about 2,800-3,000 ft an hour) as the training target. Use it only as an example of event-specific pacing. Luke Nelson's story explains using shorter races as training for a bigger goal.
- Course technicality (ridges, scrambling, snow) must be trained for, not just distance and vertical (Kílian, Ch. 13).
- `[GAP]` No race pacing strategy by distance.

### 7.5 Altitude, terrain access, time-poor athletes
- Altitude: only athlete stories mention it. No acclimatization method. `[GAP]`
- Without mountains: John Kelly's story uses short steep hills, incline treadmill, and commute running, but notes lost downhill work. Gym ME is the book's answer for no-terrain athletes (Ch. 8). `[BOOK]`

## 9. Questions to ask the athlete before planning

**Goal and event**
1. Which event or trip? Date, distance, vertical, terrain, technicality, altitude, cut-offs.
2. Is it an A, B, or C goal? Any other races in the season?
3. Sport mix: running, skimo, ski mountaineering, or all three? Poles, roller skis, or snow access?

**History and category**
4. Average weekly hours and vertical in the past 8-12 weeks, and over the last 12 months. Is there a log?
5. Years of structured endurance training; childhood or teen history.
6. Has the athlete finished an event of this type or the next step down in the last 12 months, and how did it go?
7. Typical weak points (see 6.5 and section 8): uphill, downhill, flat speed, leg fatigue, GI, technique.

**Physiology and tests**
8. Heart rate monitor type; known AeT and LT heart rates; any lab or field test; max heart rate.
9. Easy-pace behavior: does the pace at the talk or nose-breathing level feel very slow? Is there a hard-training history?
10. Recent illness, injury, or layoff. Ferritin or other blood work.

**Recovery and life**
11. Sleep, work and family stress, travel, shift work.
12. Available training hours per week; which days; time of day.
13. Current recovery signs: resting heart rate, mood, motivation, soreness.
14. Current strength habits and the self-test results (squat, step, push-up, pull-up, plank).

**Constraints and safety**
15. Terrain access (hills, snow, gym); gear.
16. Medical conditions or restrictions; need for clinician clearance.
17. Fueling and gut tolerance in long efforts (book gives little guidance).
18. Preferred coach support level; willingness to follow slow easy days.

## 10. Gaps and uncertainties

- **Publication year.** The copyright page says 2019. The foreword is dated May 2018. Citations may use either; 2019 is the publication year.
- **Missing figures and tables.** Chapter 3 zone chart (Fig. 3.3), recovery time chart (Fig. 3.4), lactate and fat-versus-carbohydrate graphs, strength exercise illustrations, transition-period figures (10.1-10.3), all macro-plan progressions (11.2-11.10), and all example weeks (12.1-12.12, 13.1-13.6) appear only as captions or notes. The percentages in the example weeks are not available. Zone boundary percentages for Z3-Z5 are missing.
- **Internal inconsistencies.**
  - Hill sprint reps: 8-10 seconds (Ch. 5, 8 implied) vs 10-30 seconds (Ch. 9).
  - Pick-ups: 8-15 seconds (Ch. 5) vs 6 x 15 to 8 x 30 seconds (Ch. 9).
  - Tempo: 20-60 minutes (Ch. 5) vs 20-40 (Ch. 9).
  - Z4 recovery: 1:1 (Ch. 9, Ch. 5 text) vs up to 2 minutes.
  - Z2 limits: "10-15 percent of weekly volume" (Ch. 3) vs "no more than 15 percent" (Ch. 11).
  - Intensity share: "80-90 percent Z1-Z2" (Ch. 5), "80-85 percent" for elites (Ch. 3), "80/6-8/12-14" (Seiler), "80/20" (glossary).
  - Recovery week drop: 50 percent (week-type text) vs 40-60 percent (progression text).
  - Weekly vertical start: one third to one half (Ch. 11) vs up to half (Ch. 13).
- **Missing method.** The "max strength" program is referenced but not found. A text search finds no "drift", "eccentric", "caffeine", or "sodium", and "HRV" appears only as a symptom.
- **Conversion artefacts.** Stray "Chapter N" headings, glued sentences from figure captions, page references with HTML anchors, and index entries with unusable page numbers. A few typos exist in the source (for example, "intesity" in the glossary). The converted `Fig. 8.1` label is reused for the box step-up in Ch. 7 and 8.
- **Dated numbers.** Lab costs, TrainingPeaks as the main tool, and study references reflect 2018 and earlier. `[INFER]` Check current tools and costs.
- **Elite and anecdote bias.** Many numbers come from Kílian, Foote, and other pros. The book itself warns against copying them.
- **Safety scope.** This file does not replace medical advice. Several book topics (overtraining, ferritin, illness, injury) need a clinician.
