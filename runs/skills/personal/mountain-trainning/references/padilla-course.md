# Padilla climbing course resources: summary

Source: a shared Drive folder of climbing-training materials by Alejandro Padilla Crespo, a Spanish climbing coach who promotes a paid program called Escalada Inteligente.
The folder holds 8 module index pages, 2 mobility PDF guides, and 2 research papers. The index pages link to 22 YouTube videos, which hold most of the teaching content. This file summarises the pages, the guides, the papers, and the captions of those videos.
The summaries are in my own words. The original files stay local and are not in the repository.

## How to use this file

- Load it for finger strength, climbing technique, climbing endurance, fear, injury prevention, mobility, and moving up a grade.
- For the books, see [synthesis.md](./synthesis.md). This course adds climbing-specific detail that *The Climbing Bible: Practical Exercises* only partly covers: tests, endurance, fear, and a roadmap by grade.

## Reading the tags

- `[SRC: ...]` is a statement from the named resource.
- `[INFER]` is an inference, not a source statement.

## Module 1: Tests for climbers

### Module overview and philosophy
- Core idea: do not train blind. Feelings mislead, so measure to find the real limiter before choosing what to train. [SRC: module 1 index]
- Tests are for better decisions, not for number obsession. The limiter may be grip strength, endurance, mobility, or non-physical: technique, tactics, fear, rhythm, rests, planning. [SRC: module 1 index]
- Climbing is multifactorial. The videos repeatedly list other factors: technique, tactics, rest management, rate of force development (RFD), pulling strength, mobility, psychology. [SRC: all three test videos]
- The author says no single best test exists. The tests shown are his team's preference. [SRC: grip strength video]
- How an agent should apply: treat the three tests as a screening battery, not a verdict. Always check technique and tactics too. [INFER]
- Questions to ask the athlete: What do you feel limits you on V4–V5 problems (fingers, power, pump, hips, fear, reading)? Have you ever tested anything objectively?

### Test of grip strength (video Vggn_gHzus0)
- Core idea: ability to hang small edges is a key limiter of performance and separates elite climbers from others. Hang time or added weight on edges of different depths shows whether finger strength is your limiter. [SRC: grip strength video]
- Relative strength matters: a lighter climber with equal absolute hold strength is relatively stronger. [SRC: grip strength video] High relative strength does not automatically mean harder sends. [SRC: grip strength video]
- Equipment: a hangboard or edges, two-hand hangs, optional added weight. A force sensor is mentioned as a more precise option but not explained. [SRC: grip strength video]
- Edge depth choices (research cited by the author):
  - Below about 6 mm, results depend more on anthropometry (finger/skin morphology) than strength, so avoid testing there. [SRC: grip strength video]
  - Edges deeper than one finger phalanx recruit the superficial finger flexor more and are less specific, especially for high-level climbers. Edges of one phalanx or less load the deep finger flexor more. [SRC: grip strength video]
- Protocol A, "MED 40" (captions garble it as "MEDET 40"; "MED" likely = minimum edge depth, [INFER]):
  1. Find the smallest edge depth on which you can hang 40 s with body weight only.
  2. On that edge, find the maximum added weight you can hold for 5 s.
  3. Divide by body weight to get relative strength. [SRC: grip strength video]
- Protocol B (study cited by the author): three tasks. (1) Maximum hang time on a 14 mm edge. (2) Edge depth held 40 s. (3) Maximum weight held 5 s on that edge. The max-weight test was reliable and valid for elite climbers and the 14 mm time test for advanced climbers. [SRC: grip strength video]
- Protocol C (López et al., 2021, as cited): tested hang time on 6, 8, 10, 12 and 14 mm. 8 mm was best overall, but only 72% of non-elite and 91% of elite climbers could hang it. 10 and 12 mm were the alternatives. [SRC: grip strength video]
- Interpretation: compare your results with the on-screen table of hang times by level (elite vs non-elite, per edge depth). The table is not in the captions, so no norms are available here. [SRC: grip strength video]
- Interpretation of relative strength against grade: the video says the numbers show whether your grip level supports your goal grade or sits below it. No threshold is stated. [SRC: grip strength video]
- Example progress, [CLAIM] (program students): MED 40 depth from 18 to 11 mm in 6 months; 25 to 15 mm with a 6B to 7A jump; 17 to 12 mm with a 7B to 7C jump. Captions garble one value ("140"). Anecdotal, no controls.
- Prerequisites and suitability: both are research on advanced and elite climbers. The video does not give a beginner caveat for this test. [SRC]
- Safety: [INFER] maximal-load hangs on small edges stress finger tendons and pulleys. For a climber under 1 year, prefer conservative loading, thorough warm-up, and low or no added weight. Mention a professional or the athlete's own judgment if finger pain exists.
- Common errors: testing on edges below 6 mm; using edges too deep to be specific; reading strength as the only limiter. [SRC]
- How an agent should apply: [INFER] for a V2–V3 athlete aiming at V5, technique and body positioning usually limit before finger strength, so test grip only if the athlete falls off on small holds despite good technique. Use a body-weight hang time on a fixed edge (e.g., 20 mm) as a safe baseline rather than max weight. Re-test every few months on identical conditions.
- Questions to ask the athlete: Do you own a hangboard and which edge depths? Any finger, pulley or elbow pain? Body weight? Do you fail on small crimps or on body position? Have you hung before?

### Test of grip endurance (video aFzKFozN6wU)
- Core idea: "I lack endurance" is vague. Test which kind of endurance limits you before adding volume. [SRC: module 1 index]
- Claims in the video: forearm flexor endurance (not running endurance) characterizes rope climbers; ability to sustain repeated contractions to failure separates elite from advanced climbers. [CLAIM] Cited as research, but no source is given.
- Two endurance types: aerobic (low intensity) and anaerobic (high intensity). The author uses the term "alactic anaerobic", which is questionable for efforts lasting minutes. [SRC] [CLAIM]
- Prerequisite: the author explicitly says these tests are demanding and not recommended for people who are just starting climbing. [SRC: endurance video]
- Setup (same as the strength test): find the MED 40 edge depth, then max 5 s hang weight on it. Max bilateral force = body weight + that added weight. Example in the video: 60 kg body weight + 40 kg = 100 kg. Test load = 80% of that (80 kg, so 20 kg added to the harness in the example). [SRC: endurance video]
- High-intensity test 1 (de-oxygenation capacity): repeat 8 s hang, 1 s rest, to failure at 80% load. Record number of sets and total time. [SRC: endurance video]
- High-intensity test 2 (re-oxygenation capacity): same 80% load, but 7 s hang and 3 s rest to failure. Captions read "73"; I read this as 7 s on / 3 s off. Uncertain. [SRC] [INFER]
- Low-intensity test: critical force, defined as the highest force you can hold almost indefinitely without fatigue. Steps are in the author's blog post, which is not provided. The author says it correlates very well with sport-climbing performance (a claim, no data). [SRC] [CLAIM]
- Interpretation: no thresholds. The author only says results show whether you are good, average, or poor, and re-tests after months show whether training worked. [SRC: endurance video]
- Safety: [INFER] 80% of maximal on a small edge to failure is a hard test with a fall-off risk (harness hang). Avoid when fatigued, injured, or with no spotter or safe space.
- How an agent should apply: [INFER] for this athlete, do not run these tests (beginner, under 1 year). Use simple field proxies instead: how many boulders in a row before failure, and whether pump or tension (gripping too hard) ends sessions. Re-evaluate after about 1–2 years of climbing.
- Questions to ask the athlete: Does fatigue end your sessions, or fear, skin, or technique? Do you pump out on long or steep problems? How much do you climb per week? Does running fitness feel different from forearm endurance?

### Test of mobility (video yMuoPYXAaA4)
- Core idea: shoulder and hip range of motion can limit performance and raise injury risk. Flexibility alone is not enough; you need range plus strength inside that range. [SRC: mobility video and index]
- Joints and movements to assess (both joints): shoulder flexion, abduction, external rotation; hip flexion, abduction, external rotation. [SRC: mobility video]
- Why it matters: hips: high feet on slabs, stepping up, wide reaches; shoulders: external rotation often limits moves and forcing range repeatedly causes shoulder injury (author's claim). [SRC] [CLAIM] about injury mechanism.
- The author says mobility is a performance differentiator and an indicator of health, citing unnamed research. [SRC] [CLAIM]
- Three steps: (1) assess; (2) specific mobility training; (3) transfer to the wall. [SRC: mobility video]
- Step 1 test details: tests appear as on-screen images and in a blog post, not in captions. Only the joint motions above are stated. [SRC]
- Step 2: the author distinguishes flexibility (static stretch, e.g., holds of 30 s to 3 min) from mobility (strength through full range). He says static stretching alone transfers less. [SRC] This is his position; the video shows no evidence.
- Examples shown (audio missing): a single-leg squat to a deep position, a hip external-rotation isometric with squeezed glutes held for seconds, and a shoulder flexion exercise. No sets, reps or durations stated. [SRC]
- Exercise selection: any of many exercises is fine if it targets the six motions above and loads end range. [SRC]
- Step 3: use climbing sessions and problems with technical moves that demand this range. [SRC]
- Key rule: apply force at the end of the range so the range gained is useful. [SRC]
- Interpretation: no numeric norms. Results are labeled good, average, or poor. [SRC]
- Safety: do not force range beyond what you can control; repeated forcing is linked to injury. [SRC] [CLAIM]
- How an agent should apply: [INFER] this athlete wants flexibility, so this video fits well. Steps: (1) ask for a simple self-screen of hip flexion/abduction/external rotation and shoulder motion (use standard physio tests; the source gives none). (2) Prescribe active, loaded end-range work (e.g., isometric holds at end range, controlled squats), not only stretching. (3) Link to heel hooks and high-foot problems. Runners and lifters often have tight hips, so check for asymmetry. Suggest seeing a physio for pain.
- Questions to ask the athlete: Can you put a foot above hip height on a slab and stand up? Hip or knee discomfort? Shoulder pain overhead? Which moves feel blocked by mobility? Current stretching or lifting routine?

## Module 4: Climbing technique

### Module overview
- Core idea: better climbing does not equal more strength. Efficiency reduces energy cost and arm reliance. Skills include flow, body position, foot use, saving force, and removing needless moves. [SRC: module 4 index]
- Author's supporting example: Adam Ondra reportedly has modest grip strength but excels by technique. Captions write "Alan/Adan Ondra". The research claim is not cited. [SRC] [CLAIM]
- How an agent should apply: [INFER] for a V2–V3 to V5 goal, technique likely yields faster gains than strength work. Prioritize it before heavy fingerboarding.

### Basic movement technique (video KwKip8Efx2Q)
- Core idea: master basic movement so you can use strength efficiently. The author says this opens the way to the 6th grade and then 7th grade (French grades). [SRC] [CLAIM]
- Fluid vs static climbing: static climbing costs more energy; holding holds longer means more force for longer and more fatigue. Use fluid, controlled, dynamic movement most of the time. Fluid climbing also uses momentum and allows long reaches impossible statically. [SRC]
- Static is acceptable when needed, e.g., delicate slabs, small holds, or technical moves. Elite climbers spend little time static (author's claim). [SRC] [CLAIM]
- Three key concepts:
  - Base of support: area between points bearing weight. Larger base means more stability but harder to shift weight; smaller base means more freedom and more risk of losing balance. [SRC]
  - Center of gravity: near the navel. [SRC]
  - Weight shifts: move the body to place the center of gravity before releasing a hand or foot. [SRC]
- Barn door ("puerta"): swinging out because you let go without adjusting the center of gravity. Fix: pre-adjust hips and feet, then move the hand. [SRC]
- Basic position: arms straight and relaxed; knees bent; hips close to the wall; use the tips of the shoes. Legs are stronger than arms, so load the legs. [SRC]
- Common errors: straight legs push all weight on the arms; bending the elbows along with the knees; hips back from the wall; advancing to hard moves before mastering basics. [SRC]
- Prerequisites: none beyond basic climbing. Good hip mobility is needed to keep hips close with bent knees. [SRC]
- Safety: none stated. [INFER] Dynamic movement on holds you cannot hold adds risk; practice controlled moves on moderate problems first.
- How an agent should apply: [INFER] have the athlete film problems and check: does the hand move before the hips? Are arms straight? Do they barn-door on traverse-style moves? Include drills: silent feet, lateral moves with hip pre-positioning, slow versus flowing runs of the same problem.
- Questions to ask the athlete: Do you notice barn-dooring? Do you tend to hang on straight arms or bent? Do you climb slowly and statically? Do you film yourself?

### Foot technique (video rURuEM5oCwo)
- Core idea: legs and feet should carry as much load as possible. Feet are underused. The foot grips like a hand. [SRC]
- Four foot actions: pull the body toward the foot, push away, balance forces, support weight. [SRC]
- Five keys for placing the shoe: (1) look at the foot onto the hold; (2) shape the foot to the hold, direction of movement and leg action; (3) use the toe tip (front, outer, or inner), not the flat shoe jammed on; (4) sometimes scrub to settle the shoe on the hold; (5) be quiet, no kicking ("like a ninja"). [SRC]
- Why use the tip: it allows up to about 180° rotation for changing feet and gives precision on tiny holds. [SRC] [CLAIM] on the exact angle.
- Parts of the shoe, uses (paraphrased):
  - Inside edge: breaks balance, can pinch like pliers, can hold a barn door.
  - Top of the foot (instep): keep it perpendicular to the hold; the calf supplies the force.
  - Heel: unloads weight and enables heel hooks.
  - Sole: for slabs and friction. [SRC]
- Foot-swap techniques on the same hold:
  - Jump: beginner favorite; needs good holds, imprecise, and leaves only the hands on the wall, which adds fatigue. [SRC]
  - Dancer: place one foot on the other then release the lower one; good on vertical and slab, poor on overhangs (slips), hard if the foot is stuck. [SRC]
  - Pivot: rotate the foot, leave space, place the other; precise, works on overhangs; the author's favorite and for somewhat advanced climbers. [SRC]
- Heel-hook errors briefly: sole flat to wall with hips far from the wall; toes pointing to the ceiling; better to point toes sideways. [SRC]
- Prerequisites: none; suits beginners. [SRC] The author says tips-not-flat is a very common novice error. [SRC]
- Safety: none stated. [INFER] Swapping and heel work on slippery overhangs may slip, so go controlled.
- How an agent should apply: [INFER] for this athlete, foot work is probably the largest quick win. Suggest silent-foot drills, eyes-on-foot habit, slab and vertical problems with forced foot swaps (dancer, pivot), and filming the feet. Check shoe fit and condition as a factor.
- Questions to ask the athlete: Do you look at your feet until they stick? Do you stomp or scrape? Do your feet slip on small holds? Which foot swap do you use? What shoes do you wear?

### Heel hook (video RmM-rJl_62Y)
- Core idea: pull and weight the heel on a hold to keep balance, step onto a high foot with less arm load, relieve bad hand holds or overhangs, compress between hands and feet, and gain reach. The author calls it advanced and complex but needed eventually. [SRC]
- The author promises five steps but the captions describe four (the fifth may be on screen or lost): (1) heel on hold, toes pointing down; (2) rotate externally, toes outward, to raise contact area and use glutes more than hamstrings; (3) active tension: pull the heel toward the hips, push with the other leg, brace the core; (4) a "column of force": keep applying force while the hands move so the heel does not slip. [SRC]
- Typical errors: leaving the heel hanging with no load, like laundry on a line (fix: toes down and pull); tiny holds forcing toes up (fix: external hip rotation); hips far from the heel (fix: bring hips closer). [SRC]
- Progressions (counts as stated by the author):
  1. Level 1, isolate: on a large volume, place the heel, pull, release one hand, about 5 reps per side; and stand up on a high heel with hips above it, about 3 reps per side. Move slowly, with steady pull. [SRC]
  2. Level 2, real context: a boulder with a mild, mandatory heel; goal is to keep tension while moving the hand. [SRC]
  3. Level 3, advanced: an overhanging wall with a chain of heel hooks and long hand crosses; squeeze with the heel on each hand move. [SRC]
- Supporting training: hip mobility in abduction, rotation, flexion, both passive (range) and active (strength in range); hip must be mobile, strong, stable. Strengthen the posterior chain (hamstrings, glutes), e.g., double-leg or single-leg glute bridge variants. [SRC]
- Safety: the author cites a recent study (captions say 2026, uncertain) that lower-limb injuries are rising with more bouldering, and heel hooks are a main mechanism. The knee rotates outward under load, stressing collateral ligament, meniscus, cruciate and others; a pop may occur if range is exceeded. Reduce risk by respecting knee range, mobility and posterior leg strength. [SRC] Study not named in captions.
- Video review: filming lets you find real errors. A coach critique example on a mantel stresses heel placement, rotating the heel, and shifting weight toward the heel instead of rising straight up. [SRC]
- Mantel feedback (paraphrased): build a small "ladder" step before the heel, place feet within body width, keep the lower foot rather than losing it. [SRC]
- How an agent should apply: [INFER] for V2–V3 climbers, introduce heel hooks gradually: start with level 1 on large volumes and low-risk positions; avoid forced or dynamic heel hooks while still developing. Pair with hip work and glute bridges already compatible with the athlete's lifting. Stop on knee pain, especially lateral or posterior knee pain.
- Questions to ask the athlete: Any knee or hamstring history? Do you use heels now? Hip mobility (can you place a heel high)? Do you lift (bridges, hamstring work)? Do you film sessions?

## Module 5: Gym strength training for climbers

Source: course by Alejandro Padilla Crespo (program "Escalada Inteligente"). Two index pages plus two video transcripts (YouTube auto-captions, so numbers may be wrong). The videos end with a pitch for the paid coaching program. [CLAIM] Client success stories (grade jumps, "600 climbers helped") are promotional and not verifiable.

### Video 5.1: Gym strength guide

**Core idea**
- [SRC: 5.1] Strength work in a gym is a required part of a climber's plan, whatever the level or discipline. Climbing only on the wall and on rock is compared to a table with a missing leg.
- [SRC: 5.1] Slow, bulky or heavy climbers result from badly designed gym work, not from strength training itself.
- [SRC: 5.1] Strength training must be fitted to level, discipline, injuries, availability, goals and time of season. It must be combined with climbing, technique, mental skills, tactics and mobility.

**Four myths and the speaker's answers**
- Myth "makes you slow": [SRC: 5.1] well-planned training with fast intent, aimed at rate of force development (RFD) and power, improves how quickly force is produced through neural adaptation. Bodybuilding-style work gives bodybuilding results.
- Myth "makes you big": [SRC: 5.1] muscle size depends on total volume, how close sets go to failure and calorie intake. Moderate volume, a neural emphasis and calories at or slightly under maintenance raise relative strength without much extra mass.
- Myth "causes injuries": [SRC: 5.1] the speaker says strength programs are the most effective way to cut sports and overuse injuries, and rate it above flexibility work, proprioception work or nutrition. [CLAIM] No reference is shown in the transcript, and the wording is strong.
- Myth "hurts quality of life": [SRC: 5.1] strength training is linked to lower mortality and cardiometabolic events and to better function over a lifetime. [CLAIM] Stated without citation.

**Evidence cited**
- [SRC: 5.1] A 2017 randomized study (authors only on screen, not named in audio) had three groups: gym strength with heavy loads and few reps, gym strength with light loads and many reps, and a climbing-only control.
- [SRC: 5.1] The gym groups trained twice a week for 10 weeks and lowered the intensity and number of their climbing sessions. The control group only climbed.
- [SRC: 5.1] Climbing-specific test scores rose about 11% and 12% in the two gym groups and 4.1% in the control group. The audio is unclear on exact figures; treat as approximate.
- [SRC: 5.1] The gym exercises did not target the finger flexors. They trained pulling, pushing and similar muscle groups.
- [SRC: 5.1] Conclusion: both heavy-low-rep and light-high-rep work can help. The choice depends on discipline, training level, goals and season.

**Method: two-step planning**
1. Assessment [SRC: 5.1]
   - Injury history and current injuries (to add strengthening or rehab-type work).
   - Days available, equipment, climbing type (slab versus steep and physical), level.
   - Tests of limiting factors that the gym can fix, such as pulling strength, pushing strength, mobility. Train the weakest link. Example: cannot do pull-ups, so work pulling; can do heavy weighted pull-ups, so move to other areas or toward one-arm work.
2. Planning [SRC: 5.1]
   - Dose: 1 session per week is the minimum effective dose, 2 is ideal, 3 is the maximum. Reduce when climbing outdoors a lot or after months of heavy gym work.
   - Exercise choice: based on level, injuries and weaknesses. Do not copy influencer exercises.
   - Stimulus type: light load with many reps leans toward muscular endurance and muscle mass. Heavy load with few reps leans toward strength and power.
   - Example for someone with little muscle and no gym habit: start with some months of light loads and high reps to learn technique and add some muscle. Later move to heavy, low-rep strength work. The speaker calls this only an example.

**Example session structure (a coached 8B+ climber)** [SRC: 5.1]
- Warm-up with shoulder mobility. Reason given: shoulders, fingers and elbows are the three main climbing injury sites.
- Core and wrist work (for example controlled concentric and eccentric dumbbell wrist work, planks).
- Shoulder stabilizer work, then pushing and pulling exercises. Elbow flexor strength is also mentioned.

**Scheduling examples** [SRC: 5.1]
- Very limited time: two gym-climbing days plus weekend rock, with a few strength exercises added at the end of the climbing session.
- Moderate time: 2 gym-climbing days, 1 gym day, 1 rock day.
- More time: 2 climbing-gym days, 2 strength days, 2 rock days.

**Common errors named** [SRC: 5.1]
- Dismissing gym work as a waste of time.
- Training like a bodybuilder.
- Copying social-media exercises that you may not be ready for or may not need.
- Ignoring injury history.

**Safety caveats**
- [SRC: 5.1] Reduce climbing intensity and volume when adding gym blocks (as the study group did) and consider season timing.
- [INFER] The source gives no loading numbers for gym exercises (sets, reps, percentage). Do not invent them from this video alone.

**How an agent should apply**
- [INFER] Treat gym strength as 1–2 sessions per week for this athlete. Start with 1 if the load of climbing, running and hangboard is already high.
- [INFER] Choose exercises from the athlete's weakest tested area, not from a generic list. With 10 strict pull-ups, pulling volume is probably not the weak link. Check pushing, core, shoulder health, single-leg strength and mobility first.
- [INFER] The athlete wants flexibility. This video treats mobility as a part of the session (shoulders as the example), and says flexibility alone is not the best injury-reduction tool. Keep mobility inside the plan as an additional element.
- [INFER] An athlete who is new to gym work and has under 1 year of climbing fits the speaker's "start light, many reps, learn technique, then go heavy" example. Running adds load on the legs, so schedule leg work away from key run days.

**Questions to ask the athlete**
- Any current or past shoulder, elbow, finger or wrist problems?
- Days per week and equipment available for gym work? Can strength be added after climbing sessions?
- Current gym experience and which lifts are comfortable?
- Weak points you notice (mobility, core, pushing, shoulder stability)?
- Running volume per week and the hardest run days?

### Video 5.2: Step-by-step to improve pull-ups

**Core idea**
- [SRC: 5.2] Pulling strength (pull-up) is one performance factor among many. Heavy pulling does not help if footwork, fear management, tactics and mobility are weak.
- [SRC: 5.2] Pulling strength matters most on overhanging walls, roofs and physical climbing. On vertical or slab climbing, mobility, technique or grip may limit more. The speaker still recommends training it for health.
- [SRC: 5.2] Max pull-up count was once used as a performance predictor. Recent studies, as described, show that pulling strength matters mainly for low and intermediate levels and becomes less of a priority at high grades. [CLAIM] No study named.
- [SRC: 5.2] Exception stated: female climbers should keep working on pulling strength over a lifetime. [CLAIM] No reason or reference given.
- [SRC: 5.2] The speaker says the method is how his team coaches, not the only valid method.

**Progression by pull-up level (the three or four tiers)**
| Level (pull-ups) | Priority and exercises (per source) |
|---|---|
| 0–3 | Learn the pull-up movement with excellent technique, with scapular retraction and no shrugged shoulders, before adding load or reps. Then build general fitness: core, pushing, leg drive (propulsion) and general strength. Add other pulling such as rows with TRX, cable, dumbbell or barbell, then progress toward inverted rows. |
| 3–8 | Eccentric pull-ups: start at the top (use a bench or jump), lower under control, add weight as you manage many reps. Also isometric holds ("locks") on the bar at different elbow angles, for example 45°, 90°, about 120° (the caption says 180°, then corrects to 120°; treat angle values as uncertain). Add weight once bodyweight locks are mastered. The speaker says these two exercises alone can raise your pull-up count. |
| More than 8 | Maximal pulling strength: weighted pull-ups with few reps and a large reserve. Example: a load that allows 5 reps while you could do about 5 more, but you stop. Aim to raise rep count and the max single-rep weight. |
| Fourth tier (lifts above about 50% of bodyweight for one rep; the captions also show 80% for the example athlete, so treat the number as uncertain) | Move toward one-arm pull-ups, then one-arm locks. Heavier two-arm work is less useful here. Training power (not max strength) is also an option. |

**Prerequisites and who it suits**
- [SRC: 5.2] Technique first for those under about 3 pull-ups. Heavy or weighted work for those above 8.
- [CLAIM] Client examples: 0 to 6 pull-ups and 5+ to 6B+; a climber moving from 7C to 8A with a one-arm pull-up. Promotional.

**Dosage logic**
- [SRC: 5.2] No sets, weekly frequency or percentages are given, apart from the "5 reps with 5 in reserve" example and the 50%/80% bodyweight references.

## Module 6: Grip (finger) strength

Source: index page plus three videos. The index page warns that more finger strength does not automatically mean a higher grade, and that bad finger training wastes time, builds fatigue and raises injury risk. The videos promote the paid program (and a force sensor method the team uses).

### Video 6.1: Traditional method (hangs)

**Core idea**
- [SRC: 6.1] Absolute strength ignores body weight. Relative strength includes it. Climbing is limited by relative strength, so the same total load on the fingers means more for a lighter climber.
- [SRC: 6.1] The video's worked example: two climbers of equal weight (60 kg). One hangs a 20 mm edge with 40 kg added, the other with 1 kg added. Given equal technique and mind, the weaker one climbs near maximum effort on a 20 mm edge, so fatigue accumulates and smaller edges cannot be held.
- [SRC: 6.1] Grip strength is a main performance limiter in the research. Still, endless max hangs month after month is an error. Other limiters include endurance, mobility, technique, tactics, mental skills and pulling strength.
- [SRC: 6.1] Tools named: wall work, boards (Moonboard, Kilter), campus, traditional hangs, force sensors. This video covers traditional hangs only.

**Requirements before starting max hangs** [SRC: 6.1]
1. At least 1–2 years of climbing experience with systematic training of about 2–4 days per week.
2. Older than 16.
3. No contraindication to strength training and no injury.
4. Solid basic movement technique. Raising finger strength without technique is called a mistake.
- If not met, work on other areas or follow the beginner approach (the speaker offers to film a beginner video if requested; see 6.3).

**Two traditional methods**
- Weighted max hangs [SRC: 6.1]: add load on a relatively large edge. Hang time example is 10 s. The load lets you hold for a chosen time with 2–3 s of reserve (up to 5 s for beginners at this work). Example: 20 mm edge, load allows about 15 s, you hang 10 s.
- Max hangs on the smallest possible edge [SRC: 6.1]: bodyweight only, on a smaller edge. Same logic: pick an edge on which you could hold about 15 s, hang 10 s.
- Note: the video's own "reserve" examples (15 s possible, 10 s done) equal 5 s of reserve, while the text says 2–3 s, or up to 5 for beginners. Treat as approximate.

**Study cited (thesis by Eva López; the audio may garble the name)** [SRC: 6.1]
- Two groups, crossover design. Group A: 4 weeks of weighted hangs on an 18 mm edge (10 s hangs), 2 weeks rest, then 4 weeks of bodyweight hangs on the smallest possible edge. Group B did the reverse order.
- Biggest grip gains came in the first 4 weeks in both groups. The group that did weighted hangs first and the smallest edge second improved more overall.
- The training grip was half crimp. The speaker reports that grip strength also improved in other grip types and that half crimp is less injury-prone than full crimp. Sample size and effect sizes are not given.

**Dosage logic and numbers (all from 6.1)**
- Hang duration example: 10 s.
- Reserve: 2–3 s, up to 5 s for people starting this work.
- Rest between sets: complete, 3–5 minutes.
- Do not train to failure. Sessions should be short and far from failure. The speaker says research shows failure training does not build strength or power. [CLAIM] No reference named in audio.
- Progression: if you have never done hangs, do a mesocycle of bodyweight hangs on the smallest edge first. Then progress to weighted hangs, starting on very large edges and reducing depth as you advance.
- Do not copy the study protocol. Adapt to level, discipline, goals and availability.

**Common errors named** [SRC: 6.1]
- Only ever training max finger strength.
- Starting with weight before a bodyweight block.
- Too little rest between sets (builds fatigue, raises injury risk, no strength gain).
- Stopping the session late or training to failure.
- Skipping technique.

**Safety caveats**
- [SRC: 6.1] Hoist loads properly: bend the knees, clip the carabiner through the belt loop (the audio says "ring"), keep a straight torso, and use a support to stand up if the load is heavy.
- [SRC: 6.1] Do not hang if injured.

**How an agent should apply**

## Module 7: Climbing endurance

### Scope and core idea (video "Cómo mejorar tu resistencia en el muro")
- Core idea: endurance is not "more routes". It is a chosen type of work, with intensity, duration, rest and format set on purpose. [SRC: M7 index + video]
- The video covers only local endurance of the finger-flexor forearm muscles, not running or cycling endurance. [SRC: M7 video]
- It targets rope/sport climbing most. The speaker says the ability to keep intermittent contractions going until exhaustion separates beginners from advanced and elite climbers. [SRC: M7 video]
- A 2009 study (author name garbled in captions, probably Vanesa España-Romero and colleagues) compared time to exhaustion on a wall. Elite climbers lasted longer. [SRC: M7 video] Name and details are uncertain.
- Less fatigue leaves more capacity to think, decide and link moves. [SRC: M7 video]
- Symptoms the module lists: early forearm pump, no margin at the crux, quality falling during the route. [SRC: M7 index]

### The two endurance types and what each trains
- Low intensity (aerobic): the speaker says it raises blood flow, capillary density, new vessel growth, mitochondria and energy production. [SRC: M7 video]
- Adaptations only happen if there is no acidity and no pump. [SRC: M7 video]
- High intensity: trains anaerobic glycolysis, clearing of waste products during hard climbing, and clearing during rest. [SRC: M7 video]
- High-intensity work must create fatigue products but must stop short of failure. Failure raises injury risk and lengthens recovery. [SRC: M7 video]
- The captions call the high-intensity system "anaerobic alactic". That label conflicts with the glycolysis and waste-product content, so it is probably a speaker slip or caption error. [CLAIM] Do not repeat the label.
- The aerobic base supports the high-intensity work, so training only one type is called an error. [SRC: M7 video]
- Intensity (low, medium, high) and format (continuous or intermittent) are the two variables to change. [SRC: M7 video]

### Method A: low-intensity aerobic work on a wall
- Target: under 30% of maximum finger-flexor grip force, attributed to "scientific research" with no citation. [SRC: M7 video] [CLAIM] on the source.
- On a wall you cannot measure that. The speaker gives proxies: slight forearm swelling and redness, never a pump. On a 1–5 effort scale, aim near 2 (5 = pump). [SRC: M7 video]
- Continuous format: 5 minutes up to 30, 45 or 60 minutes without leaving the wall. [SRC: M7 video]
- Intermittent format: sets of 4, 5, 10 or up to 20 minutes, with 45 s to a maximum of about 2 minutes rest. Do 2 sets up to 8 for advanced climbers. [SRC: M7 video]
- Duration by level, per the speaker:
  - Complete beginner: climb 1–2 minutes, come down, repeat, always before pump. [SRC: M7 video]
  - Beginner: 8–12 minutes. [SRC: M7 video]
  - Intermediate: 15–30 minutes. [SRC: M7 video]
  - High level: more than 40 minutes. [SRC: M7 video]
  - Elite or long-route climbers: 1 hour or more. [SRC: M7 video]
- Terrain: vertical wall with big holds. Stronger climbers add slight angle and use medium holds, without overdoing it. [SRC: M7 video]

### Method B: high-intensity local endurance
- Goal: recover better before and after a hard section. [SRC: M7 video]
- Intensity is set by wall angle and hold size, and "hard" is relative to the climber. [SRC: M7 video]
- Protocol 1, hard work plus active rest: climb a hard problem of about 6–8 moves (captions also say 3–4, so the exact number is uncertain). Without leaving the wall, climb about 1 minute on a vertical wall with big holds. Come down and repeat. [SRC: M7 video] Number of repeats is not stated.
- Adjust the rest: 1 minute or longer, a steeper wall or medium holds. [SRC: M7 video]
- Protocol 2, "4x4" with passive rest: pick a hard problem and climb it 4 times with no rest. By the 4th, forearms should be very pumped, close to failure but not failing. Then rest passively 2–4 minutes. [SRC: M7 video]
- The number of rounds, and the exact meaning of the second "4", are not stated. [SRC: M7 video] Treat the 4x4 label as ambiguous.
- Moonboard or a bouldering wall can be used, depending on level. [SRC: M7 video]

### Prerequisites, suitability, common errors, safety
- Suits: climbers who pump out early on routes or long problems. Aerobic work suits every level because it is low load. [INFER]
- Common errors named: training one endurance type only, climbing volume without a goal, drifting into pump during "aerobic" work, going to failure in high-intensity work. [SRC: M7 index + video]

### Four pillars named

### The five keys ("Las 5 claves")
1. Do not improvise. Planning each session on arrival is compared to playing the lottery. [SRC: M9 five keys]

4. Injuries as a quality indicator. Frequency and severity signal flaws in keys 1–3. [SRC: M9 five keys] [CLAIM] strong generalisation; some injuries are accidents.
5. Strategy on rock. A weekend climber gets about 60 usable days a year, and perhaps under an hour of actual climbing per rock day. Plan which days are volume (aerobic), onsight, or projecting. [SRC: M9 five keys] [CLAIM] day counts are the speaker's own estimate.

### Load-management ideas collected

### Force sensor video ("La herramienta definitiva...")
- Setup: a Tindeq sensor, a handle with cord and carabiner, an anchor point, the app. Tare the sensor with the handle hanging. [SRC: M9 sensor] Other brands exist.
- Peak Load: maximum one-hand pull with finger flexors. Use it also to check fatigue: if a pull after warm-up is clearly below your usual value (example 500 N, example reading 57.2 kg), you may be fatigued, so reconsider training. [SRC: M9 sensor] Values are examples only.
- Metrics: kilos or newtons. Data can be saved. [SRC: M9 sensor]
- RFD (rate of force development): tested before a plan, and sometimes in sessions. RFD at 200 ms is said to correlate with performance and to separate high-level boulderers. [SRC: M9 sensor] Source studies are not named.
- Repeaters: intermittent hangs at a precise percentage of your max. Example: 4 reps of 7 s on, 3 s off at 70%, 3 sets per hand, 2 min between sets. Left-hand 55 kg and right 57 kg were demo values. [SRC: M9 sensor] Not a recommendation.
- Critical Force test: said to correlate with sport-climbing performance and "almost obligatory" before planning for sport climbers. [SRC: M9 sensor] [CLAIM] no data shown.
- Other modes: live data, endurance (rarely used by the team). [SRC: M9 sensor]
- Technique used: stand under the sensor and pull vertically, shoulders down and scapulae together (not shrugged), elbow extended, neutral wrist. Let body weight settle, then pull fast and hard. Other positions have trade-offs. [SRC: M9 sensor]
- Value for prevention: control intensity, detect fatigue, compare hands, adjust with objective data. [SRC: M9 index]
- Student results (0.74 to 0.89 relative strength, about 20%; another 26%) are testimonials with garbled units. [CLAIM]

### Prerequisites, suitability, common errors, safety
- Suits everyone. The sensor suits climbers who can warm up and pull in a controlled way. [INFER]
- Errors: improvising, copying plans, skipping tests, ignoring rest, training through recurring pain. [SRC: M9 five keys + why-injured]
- Safety: maximal pulls and high-percentage hangs load fingers and pulleys. A climber under 1 year, with tendons still adapting, should get gradual progression and a skilled review of technique before testing max. [INFER]
- The sources give no treatment advice for a current injury. Refer to a clinician. [INFER]

### How an agent should apply
- Treat injury risk as a load problem: list weekly climbing, lifting and running, and look for sudden jumps. [INFER]
- Choose a few simple tests with retest dates before prescribing intensity. Without a sensor, use easier tests: same warm-up, same problem, and film it. [INFER]
- Put strength training in the plan, with moderate loads and full rest. Keep mobility work for movement goals. [INFER]
- Stop or reduce load when pain recurs or one finger, elbow or shoulder keeps hurting. [INFER]
- Do not advise max-effort finger testing for this athlete at first. [INFER]

### Questions to ask the athlete
- Any current or past injuries (fingers, elbows, shoulders)? When and how did they start?
- How many climbing, lifting and running sessions per week, and how many rest days?
- Do you warm up, and do you record sessions?
- Do you have a plan, or do you pick workouts on arrival?
- Do you own a sensor or hangboard? What is your experience with it?

## Module 10: Keys to climbing a higher grade

Source note: a short index page plus five video transcripts (Spanish auto-captions) by Alejandro Padilla Crespo, "Escalada Inteligente". The index page argues that climbers need better-directed training, not more training. Every video also promotes the coach's paid online program. This summary ignores the sales content except for this note. Grades are French. Neither the roadmap videos nor the case studies state whether a grade refers to routes or boulders, and many cases are sport routes. [SRC: index page; all videos]

Evidence quality: the roadmaps are the coach's own system, tested on his clients (he cites 400+ climbers in some videos and 600+ in others). The case studies are testimonials chosen by the seller. There are no controls and no published data. Treat every outcome as a [CLAIM].

### The factor model used throughout (context for both roadmaps)

- Performance limiters fall into three groups: physical, technical-tactical, psychological. [SRC: 6→7 video; 7→8 video]
- Physical, local (finger flexors): maximum grip strength, finger endurance, and rate of force development (RFD). Maximum grip matters more as grades rise because holds get smaller. Endurance discriminates elite sport climbers. RFD matters most for bouldering and for single hard moves on routes.
- Physical, general: pulling strength, pushing strength, core and stability, leg drive, and mobility. Hip and shoulder mobility get special mention. Pull-up strength matters less for performance on vertical or slab terrain, but it still matters for health.
- Technical-tactical: movement efficiency, reading and visualising routes, split-second decisions, and managing pace and rests.
- Psychological: fear management, self-efficacy (belief in being able to send), and stress management.
- The coach calls the base of a climber a "three-pillar" structure. For 6→7 the pillars are technique, psychology, and then physical work in parallel. For 7→8 they are technique, fear management, and key physical factors.

### The 6th-to-7th grade roadmap

Core logic: first test where you actually are. Then build a base of volume at lower grades. Only then start projecting the target grade. [SRC: 6→7 video]

**Step 0: assessment.**
- Run a test battery covering every limiter: grip strength, endurance, RFD, mobility, pulling strength, technique, tactics, psychology.
- Then adapt the plan to the climber's availability, equipment, and goals. Generic free or cheap plans fail because they skip this step.

**Gate 1 (a yes/no question, measured over the last 6 months only).** The question is whether you have both of these:
- more than 20 routes or problems from 6a to 6a+ climbed onsight, and
- more than 10 from 6b to 6b+ climbed in fewer than 5 tries.

The window is deliberate. It measures current performance, not what you did at age 20.

If the answer is no, do this weekly:
- 1–2 gym strength days, for general strength, injury resilience, and mobility.
- 2 climbing-gym training days with analytical work on fingers, strength, endurance, RFD, technique, tactics, and strategy.
- 1 rock day, ideally 2, if the goal is rock grades. If the goal is indoor grades, spend that time indoors.

Priorities at gate 1, in order:
1. Movement technique, taking most of the time. Use a technique course if you have one. Otherwise film weekly sessions and have a coach, a better friend, or yourself review them.
2. Onsight climbing: visualisation, decisions, and pace and rest management.
3. Psychology and fear management. If fear is a real limiter, it moves up to priority 2 and onsight becomes 3.
4. Projecting, deliberately the lowest priority at this stage.

Expected result: you reach the point of starting to project your first 6c and 6c+. Time: about 6–12 months, depending on starting point, availability, equipment, and rock access.

**Gate 2.** The question is whether you have both of these in the last 6 months:
- more than 30 routes or problems from 6a up to about 6b+ climbed onsight, and
- more than 5 from 6c to 6c+ climbed in fewer than 5 or 10 tries.

The captions are garbled for the exact numbers. [SRC: 6→7 video]

If the answer is no, keep the same weekly frequency. Priorities change to:
1. Technique, still.
2. Serious projecting: learn the strategy and pick somewhat harder projects.
3. Psychology.
4. Onsight, because the climber should already be competent at it by now.

If the answer is yes, begin projecting a first 7a.

**Why build volume below the target grade.** The coach's argument has three parts:
- It builds technique, tactics, onsight skill, and mental skills.
- It gives tissues time to adapt, which lowers injury risk.
- Skipping from 6a straight to 7a loses the experience of the grades in between. The coach says this is allowed but not recommended.

**Time and mindset.** The overall journey takes from about 6 months to 2 years. Three rules close the video: be patient, build the base before chasing the grade, and trust the process. He also says the method is not magic and not the only possible one.

**Anecdotes cited (all [CLAIM]).**
- A 6b climber with low pulling and grip values reached 7a after accumulating lots of volume at 6b to 6c+.
- A 6b+ climber who trained randomly reached 7c sport and 7b trad after structured training.

### The 7th-to-8th grade roadmap

Same structure as above, shifted one grade band up. The video states the first target is 8a (route or boulder, unspecified). [SRC: 7→8 video]

**Step 0: assessment and constraints.**
- Run initial climbing-specific tests of grip strength, endurance, technique, tactics, and psychology.
- Record how many days per week you can use the gym, the climbing gym, and the rock. Record which crags you can reach.
- Set realistic goals from the test results, availability, and resources. Copying a friend's plan or a free download fails because it matches someone else's weaknesses.

**Gate 1 (last 6 months).** The question is whether you have both of these:
- more than 20 routes or problems from 7a to 7a+ climbed onsight or within about 3 tries on the same day, and
- more than 10 from 7b to 7b+ in fewer than 10 tries.

The coach says this builds a solid base. A big gap, such as 7a onsight but 8a only after many tries, is the climber's own choice. He prefers a climber who is good at both onsight and redpoint skills. He also warns that jumping grades without a base costs learning and injury protection, and he names finger, shoulder, and elbow pain as typical consequences.

If the answer is no, do this weekly:
- 1–2 gym strength days.
- 2 climbing-gym days.
- 1–2 rock days. One rock day per week is the stated minimum effective dose for improving on rock.

Priorities:
1. Advanced movement technique (not basic technique, which is assumed from the 6→7 stage). Send weekly videos of indoor and rock climbing for review.
2. Onsight skills, or priority 3 if you prefer redpointing. Onsight needs good fear management, breathing, tactics, pace, and rests. These differ from redpoint skills.
3. Fear management, ideally with psychological support.
4. Projecting strategy. Write down projects and dated goals. His example: three 7b routes in a day within 2 months, then another 7b in 4 days, then a 7c in 6 sessions by month 5 or 6. He warns against projects that are far away, out of season, or lack a partner.

Expected result: you can project first 7c and 7c+. Time: 6–18 months. A few motivated, high-level, highly available clients did it in 6 months.

**Gate 2 (last 6 months).** The question is whether you have both of these:
- more than 30 routes or problems from 7a to 7b climbed onsight or within about 3 tries, and
- more than 5 from 7b+ to 7c+ in fewer than 10 tries.

If the answer is no, keep the same weekly frequency. Priorities:
1. Advanced technique, which can be improved indefinitely.
2. Projecting, now central. His program has about seven recorded lessons on this alone.
3. Fear management.
4. Onsight, now low priority because 8a is expected to be climbed as a redpoint.

Time: again 6–18 months.

**Gate 3 (before projecting a first 8a).** The coach gives physical benchmark values to check. Treat the numbers as uncertain because of the captions.
- More than 10 strict pull-ups, or a one-repetition pull-up max above 50% of body weight. It is unclear if this means added load, though the wording suggests so. Especially relevant for physical routes or bouldering.
- Single-arm maximum grip, measured by a force sensor, above about 0.80 (units garbled: possibly force per body weight per hold size). Do not rely on this number.
- Aerobic and high-intensity endurance should not be limiting.
- For boulderers, RFD above about 10,000 N/s.

Plus non-physical requirements:
- Project strategy.
- Advanced technique.
- A long-term mindset, meaning training for years, not abandoning plans after one bad session.
- Climber-specific nutrition.
- Advanced strategies such as a 7–15 day taper before a send attempt, which cuts volume and frequency but keeps intensity high.

The coach claims it is "impossible" not to send a first 8a if these hold. This is an overclaim [CLAIM]. Overall time is from about 6 months to 2 years or more. He also tells viewers to avoid internet gurus who promise fast results.

**Anecdotes cited (all [CLAIM]).** Two 7c climbers sent first 8a routes, one living in Germany and training online and one with a busy job. A third (Alejandro Roa) went from 7c to first 8a routes after a program with diagnosis, a progressive plan, weekly adjustments, video corrections, and load control. His starting problems were a plateau, fear of injury, and training by intuition.

### Case study: Juan (Juanma), 8b to first 8b+ with a newborn and a job

[CLAIM] Anecdotal and from the coach's channel. [SRC: yXZpLOJm4YE]

- **Situation:** advanced self-trained climber who reached about 8b and often got injured near peak fitness. He then had to stop, adapt, and restart. He had a full-time job and a newborn, so he had little time and poor sleep, and his rock days were short.
- **Limiting factors found:** aerobic capacity and high-intensity endurance. Further issues from his own account: no structured plan, unsuitable exercise selection, and poor rest between exercises and sessions.
- **Method:**
  1. Initial test battery plus context: job, schedule, facilities, rock access, injury history, and goals.
  2. Realistic goals: first 8b+, consolidating 8a+ and 8b.
  3. First training blocks aimed at the weak factors.
  4. In parallel, a rock strategy with short-, medium-, and long-term projects. It included when to collect volume and when to project.
  5. Daily, weekly, and monthly contact with a coach to adjust for missed weeks, rain, and shoulder pain.
- **Result (6 months, then continued for 1.5 years):**
  - Strict pull-ups rose from 19 to 22.
  - One-rep pull-up max rose by about 10 kg.
  - Unilateral maximum grip strength rose about 33%, even though it was not a limiter. Only one training block targeted it.
  - No injuries.
  - First 8b+ routes. Routes at 7c+ to 8a+ needed fewer tries, some flashed.
  - He trains without marathon sessions or seven-day weeks.

Brief extra anecdotes in the same video: a woman over 40 boulderer went from 7b to 7c bouldering. A woman stuck at 5+ because of fear reached 6b+ after fear work. [CLAIM]

### Case study: Nicolás, 7c to first 8a with a 40% grip strength gain

[CLAIM] Anecdotal. [SRC: uEVS36w6opk; also referenced in 7→8 video]

- **Situation:** a trained sport scientist who self-trained for years. He chose sessions he liked, not what he needed. He had no accountability or monitoring. He hovered between 7b and 7c on rock, and repeatedly trained up to a peak, got injured, and restarted.
- **Limiting factors found:** weak aerobic capacity, grip strength, and flexibility. He was strong in pulling and in high-intensity endurance.
- **Method:**
  - Full assessment, then context analysis (time, work, family, rock access, travel time) and realistic goal setting (first 8a).
  - The weak factors went first and the strong ones later.
  - Grip block 1: 3 weeks of 2 sessions per week. Each session had a warm-up, then maximal weighted hangs, then hard Moon-board problems of 6–8 moves with 3–5 min rest.
  - A deload week followed. Sessions and volume dropped but intensity stayed, so fatigue could clear and performance could rise.
  - Grip block 2: 3 more weeks of 2 sessions. Each had a warm-up, hangs measured with a force sensor, and problems copying the hard moves of his current project. Another deload week followed.
  - In total, about 8 weeks of maximal grip work with two protocols.
  - In parallel, a rock plan: a period of high volume for endurance, a period for tactics and onsight, and then a period of projecting 8a.
  - Short-, medium-, and long-term projects.
  - A projecting course.
- **Result (first 6 months):**
  - Max grip strength up 40% (the measurement method is not stated).
  - Pull-ups rose from 24 to 30 and he started one-arm pull-ups.
  - No injury, with no forced stop.
  - First 8a, in roughly 10–15 tries by his own message.
  - The onsight grade and the speed of repeating lower routes improved. The onsight grade is garbled in the transcript ("7 de más").

### Case study: Pablo, 6c to 7b+ after shoulder surgery

[CLAIM] Anecdotal. [SRC: TgupSkFoIpg]

- **Situation:** a healthcare worker with 24-hour shifts, a family, and little time. He self-trained, plateaued at 6c with an occasional 7a, then dislocated his shoulder, which was poorly rehabilitated. After a second dislocation he needed surgery. He lost his confidence, had a fear of falling, and felt anxious about body awareness when pulling hard.
- **Limiting factors found:** very poor endurance. He also had no plan for rock days, plus the shoulder and fear problems.
- **Method:**
  1. Initial testing, then availability, injury history, context, and realistic goals.
  2. An anatomical adaptation block, then an aerobic-capacity block. Strength work ran in parallel to protect and fully recover the shoulder.
  3. Rock plan: first aerobic work indoors, then a high volume of rock, about 10 onsight routes per day, to build reading, tactics, and rest management. Next came 6c, then 6c+, and only later 7th-grade projects. The captions are garbled on the exact block order.
  4. Work with a sport psychologist on fear.
  5. Continuous coach follow-up to adjust intensity, load, and volume, with video review and calls. Mobility, scapular work, and yoga continued.
  6. Later goal setting mixed onsight and redpoint depending on the crag.
- **Result:** shoulder reported at 100%, no limiting injuries, improved onsight grade, and a redpoint top of 7b+. A quoted figure of "85" routes is unclear in the captions (possibly total 7th-grade routes). The coach says he now exceeds his pre-surgery level.

### Cross-cutting principles

- **Assessment first.** The same message appears in all five videos. Without tests you do not know the limiter, and you may train the wrong thing. [SRC: all videos]
- **Individualisation.** Plans must reflect test results, weekly availability, equipment, rock access, injury history, work, family, and goals. Different clients followed different plans. [SRC: case videos]
- **Multifactorial performance.** Physical, technical-tactical, and psychological factors all limit grade. Strength cannot compensate for fear or poor technique. [SRC: 6→7 and 7→8 videos]
- **Weak factors first.** Both Nicolás and Juan started with their weakest factors. Pablo began with adaptation and endurance. [SRC: case videos]
- **Load management.** Cycle hard blocks with deloads that cut volume and frequency but keep intensity. Monitor frequently and adapt for life events. The repeated peak-then-injury cycle is the common pattern the coach says structure fixes. [SRC: Nicolás, Juan, Pablo]
- **Volume below the target grade.** Counts of routes at lower grades are used as readiness gates. They protect tissues and build skills. [SRC: roadmaps]
- **Rock strategy.** Plan rock days as carefully as indoor sessions. Use short-, medium-, and long-term projects. Decide when to collect volume, when to train onsight skills, and when to project. [SRC: Juan, Nicolás, Pablo]
- **Consistency and accountability.** Self-trainers tend to do what they like and skip what they need. The coach stresses patience and sustained work over months and years. [SRC: all videos]

### How an agent should apply this to a beginner at V2–V3 wanting V5

## Research paper 1: grip strength training protocols

### Reference

- [SRC: paper 1] Ruiz-López, A., Padilla-Crespo, A., & Bustamante-Sánchez, Á. (2025). Comparative analysis of two grip strength training protocols in experienced climbers. *Apunts Educación Física y Deportes*, issue 161, pp. 32-40. Published 1 July 2025 (received Sept 2024, accepted Jan 2025). Open access. DOI 10.5672/apunts.2014-0983.es.(2025/3).161.04.
- [SRC: paper 1] Affiliations: Universidad Pablo de Olavide (Seville) and Universidad Europea de Madrid. Funded by an internal competitive project of Universidad Europea de Madrid. Ethics approval from the same university.

### Question and design

- [SRC: paper 1] Main question: does 4 weeks of weighted two-handed dead hangs (called HIMA, "holding" isometrics) improve finger strength as much as active one-handed pulls on a force sensor (called PIMA, "pushing" isometrics)? The authors expected the sensor method to be better.
- [SRC: paper 1] Secondary question: does one-handed sensor training shrink the strength gap between dominant and non-dominant hand?
- [SRC: paper 1] Recruitment: 20 climbers who trained twice a week in a climbing group. Only 11 finished (45% loss; the part I read gives no reasons). Final groups: control/dead-hang n = 5 and sensor n = 6. 9 of 11 were men. Mean age about 32 years.
- [SRC: paper 1] Inclusion criteria: at least 2 years of climbing, at least 3 months in the group, previous dead-hang experience, aged 18 or older, no injury. Actual mean climbing experience was about 5 years (SD about 3). Best redpoint averaged about 14.5 on the IRCRA scale, which the authors call an intermediate level.
- [SRC: paper 1] Allocation: stratified random sampling by self-reported level. Study ran May to July 2023.
- [SRC: paper 1] Protocol shared by both groups: 4 weeks, 2 sessions per week (Monday and Wednesday), same coach, same warm-up. The warm-up included mobility, band work, 70-80 easy climbing moves, and progressive hangs down to 14 mm.
- [SRC: paper 1] Volume: 3 sets in week 1, 4 sets in weeks 2 and 3, 5 sets in week 4. Each set was a 10 s effort on a 14 mm wooden edge, with 3 min rest. Intensity was 85% of the pre-test maximum. Half-crimp or open grip allowed.
- [SRC: paper 1] Dead-hang group: two hands, load set from 85% of maximum total weight (body weight plus added weight). Sensor group: one hand at a time, pulling on a Chronojump force sensor, 85% of each hand's maximum force (in newtons).
- [SRC: paper 1] Measures before and after: maximum hang time on a 14 mm edge (MHT_14, one attempt), maximum added weight held for 5 s (ST, two hands), and peak force and RFD at 200 ms (rate of force development) per hand on the sensor (mean of 3 attempts).
- [SRC: paper 1] Statistics: two-factor mixed ANOVA (time × group) with eta squared as effect size, Bonferroni post hoc tests, paired t-tests for hand differences.

### Main results

- [SRC: paper 1] Both groups improved significantly from pre to post on all four measures (p ≤ .05). The authors conclude both methods work in 4 weeks.
- [SRC: paper 1] Peak force, dominant hand: dead-hang group about 320 to 372 N (+16%), sensor group about 362 to 432 N (+19%). Non-dominant hand: about 316 to 364 N (+15%) versus about 331 to 410 N (+24%). Percent changes are my arithmetic from the reported means.
- [SRC: paper 1] RFD at 200 ms, dominant hand: about 457 to 602 N/s (+32%) in dead-hang group versus about 579 to 719 N/s (+24%) in sensor group. Non-dominant: about 512 to 626 N/s versus about 545 to 678 N/s (+22% and +24%).
- [SRC: paper 1] MHT_14: dead-hang group 11.8 to 23.0 s; sensor group 33.0 to 39.5 s. ST (added weight): dead-hang group 6.2 to 15.4 kg; sensor group 19.5 to 21.2 kg.
- [SRC: paper 1] The group × time interaction was not significant for any measure (p from .124 to .939). The authors report eta-squared values of .242 for MHT_14, .081 for peak force in the non-dominant hand, .045 for dominant peak force, .034 for ST, and below .01 for RFD. They describe the MHT_14 value as medium to large.
- [SRC: paper 1] The authors admit the hypothesis that the sensor method would be better was not met. They say results "hint" at better outcomes with active pulls but call for caution.
- [INFER] The effect sizes quoted match the non-significant interaction rows in the table. They show a possible trend, not a demonstrated difference. A reader should not treat "medium to large" as proof that one method wins.
- [SRC: paper 1] Hand asymmetry: no significant difference between hands in any test. Dominant hand was about 5.5% stronger before and 3.9% stronger after (whole sample). Authors say the sensor method seems to reduce the gap but call the analysis superficial and the differences small.

### Limitations

- [SRC: paper 1] Stated by authors: very small sample, so no generalisation. Climbing level fairly low versus advanced climbers. Little data on participants' climbing habits. Few women. Authors note recruiting advanced climbers for a 4-week trial is hard.
- [SRC: paper 1] Stated: the hand-asymmetry analysis compares only group means and gaps are small.
- Noticed: the groups were not equal at the start. The sensor group already held more added weight (about 19.5 versus 6.2 kg) and hung longer (about 33 versus 12 s) before training. The dead-hang group had more room to improve on those tests. A time × group comparison on such data is weak.
- Noticed: no non-training group. Gains include learning effects on the tests, the shared coached climbing sessions (maximum strength focus), and normal climbing. MHT_14 used a single attempt, which adds noise.
- Noticed: 45% did not complete the study. Reasons and possible injuries are not described in the text I read. Completers may differ from dropouts.
- Noticed: the testing favours neither method cleanly. Sensor group trained and was measured on the same device. Dead-hang group trained on the same movement as the hang tests.
- Noticed: the course author (Padilla-Crespo) is a co-author, and the sensor is promoted as useful for coaches. The authors report no conflict of interest.
- Noticed: Chronojump software is free and the device is from a small vendor, so a commercial bias is not evident. The link between the authors and the course is still real.
- Noticed: participants were far more experienced (at least 2 years, mean about 5) than a climber with under 1 year. Results do not describe tendon or pulley tolerance in novices.
- Noticed: intensity is 85% of a 5 s maximum held for 10 s. Authors do not discuss injury risk or progression for less trained fingers.
- Noticed: absolute forces (about 320-430 N) were lower than in other cited studies, which the authors link to level and measurement method.

### Practical implications for an agent

- [INFER] Weight: low-to-moderate for a V2-V3 climber with under 1 year of climbing. The population is 2+ year climbers with hangboard experience.
- [INFER] Safe takeaway: a 4-week, twice-weekly, low-set-count block of maximal isometric finger work can raise finger strength in trained recreational climbers. It does not show the user needs a sensor. The user's hangboard gives passive hangs, which this paper treats as an effective baseline.
- [INFER] Do not claim sensor pulls beat hangboard hangs. The paper did not show that.
- [INFER] Do not copy the dose directly. 85% of maximum for 10 s, 3-5 sets, is a hard prescription from experienced climbers. For a user under 1 year, start well below that. Use a bigger edge, low volume, and slow progression. Use other sources in this course for the beginner dose.
- [INFER] The set progression (3, then 4, then 5 sets over 4 weeks, with 3 min rest) is a reasonable template for how to ramp volume. Treat it as an example, not a rule.
- [INFER] The hand-asymmetry finding is minor. A gap under about 5-10% is normal and not a reason to change training.
- [INFER] If the user has no sensor, an agent can still use the paper to justify measuring progress by added weight or hang time on a fixed edge. Test rarely, at the same time, after rest.
- [INFER] Suggested wording for the user: "A small study of experienced climbers found both hangboard hangs and sensor pulls raised finger strength within 4 weeks. It could not tell which is better."
- [INFER] Do not use this paper alone to set load, edge size, or weekly frequency for a climber with under 1 year of climbing. Combine it with the course material and conservative rules.
- [INFER] If the user reports finger, pulley, or elbow pain, ignore this paper's progression and advise rest and, if pain persists, a clinician.

### Evidence strength rating

- [INFER] Low. Eleven completers, no untrained control, unequal baselines, 4 weeks, non-significant between-method difference, and a more experienced population than the user.

## Research paper 2: psychophysiological response, advanced vs beginner climbers

### Reference

- [SRC: paper 2] Padilla-Crespo, A., Clemente-Suárez, V. J., & Bustamante-Sánchez, Á. (2025). Psychophysiological response differences between advanced and beginner climbers and fatigue management. *Journal of Functional Morphology and Kinesiology* (MDPI), 10(1), 50. Received 17 Oct 2024, accepted 26 Jan 2025, published 28 Jan 2025. Open access, CC BY. DOI 10.3390/jfmk10010050.
- [SRC: paper 2] Affiliation: Universidad Europea de Madrid (plus Universidad de la Costa, Colombia, for one author). Funded by an internal university project. Authors declare no conflicts of interest.

### Question and design

- [SRC: paper 2] Two aims. First: compare psychological and physiological responses to climbing between novice and advanced climbers. Second: find which test best shows fatigue after climbing. Authors expected advanced climbers to show lower anxiety and that finger-grip loss would be the best fatigue marker.
- [SRC: paper 2] Design: single-day, pre-post, two-group comparison. No training intervention.
- [SRC: paper 2] Sample: 60 lead climbers. 33 "experienced" (more than 1 year of experience, mean lead experience about 6 years, mean IRCRA 16.3, age about 33) and 27 "beginners" (about 1 month of experience, IRCRA 10, age about 22). 11 women and 49 men. All had to be able to lead climb.
- [SRC: paper 2] Procedure: standard warm-up (mobility, vertical wall, a route 2-3 grades below on-sight level, 10 repeaters of 7 s on / 3 s off on a 20 mm rung). Then initial tests. Then one on-sight lead sport route at the climber's usual on-sight grade. Then the same tests again right after descent.
- [SRC: paper 2] Tests: finger maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) on a 20 mm rung attached to a Chronojump sensor (3 alternating 5 s attempts per hand, 1 min rest), plus RFD at 200 ms, force at 200 ms, and time to 95% of MVC. Squat jump and standing long jump. Critical flicker fusion threshold (CFFT, a proxy for central fatigue). Reaction time. Heart rate and heart rate variability (Polar H10). Questionnaires: CSAI-2R (cognitive and somatic anxiety, self-confidence), STAI, and a 0-100 perceived stress scale.
- [SRC: paper 2] Statistics: MANOVA, p < 0.05, partial eta squared. No sample size calculation reported in the text I read.

### Main results

- [SRC: paper 2] Finger strength: experienced climbers were much stronger. Dominant-hand MVC about 487 N before and 453 N after climbing (about -7%). Beginners about 291 N before and 284 N after, with no significant change. Non-dominant hand: experienced about 514 N before and 463 N after (about -10%). Beginners about 320 N and 312 N. Percent changes are my arithmetic.
- [SRC: paper 2] Force at 200 ms also fell after the route in experienced climbers (about 352 to 311 N dominant, about 379 to 331 N non-dominant). Beginners did not change significantly. RFD at 200 ms fell in the non-dominant hand of experienced climbers (about 975 to 698 N/s; the table gives an implausible spread for the pre value).
- [SRC: paper 2] Reported MVC group effects: F about 8.0, p about .006, partial eta squared about .12 (dominant); F about 11.8, p about .001, partial eta squared about .17 (non-dominant). The F column is not clearly labelled in the table, so I cannot say which effect it tests.
- [SRC: paper 2] Lower-body tests (squat jump, long jump) and reaction time did not change after climbing in either group. Authors conclude they are not useful for detecting fatigue in sport climbing.
- [SRC: paper 2] CFFT: beginners dropped from about 41.2 to 39.8 and were marked as significantly different pre to post. The authors read this as central fatigue and reduced information processing. Experienced climbers stayed about 37 to 36.6.
- [SRC: paper 2] Psychology: perceived stress fell in experienced climbers (about 35 to 29) and rose in beginners (about 44 to 49); F about 4.9, p about .03, partial eta squared about .078. Somatic anxiety was about 13.5 to 11.6 in experienced climbers versus 14.7 to 14.6 in beginners (flagged significant in the table, but the listed omnibus p is .179). Cognitive anxiety, self-confidence, and STAI scores were similar between groups and low overall.
- [SRC: paper 2] Heart rate rose from about 90 bpm before to about 140-147 bpm during the route and fell to about 96 bpm after, in both groups. Heart rate variability measures fell during the route and partly recovered. Authors report some group differences (for example lower RMSSD before climbing in experienced climbers, which they read as better stress handling).
- [SRC: paper 2] Authors' conclusion: an MVC test on a 20 mm edge, especially the non-dominant hand, plus the loss of strength or RFD after a route, appears valid for assessing forearm fatigue in advanced climbers.

### Limitations

- [SRC: paper 2] Stated by authors: sample size may be a limitation; the route may not have been challenging enough (low anxiety scores in both groups); the setting was recreational, not competitive; results may not apply to speed climbing.
- Noticed: cross-sectional and single session. It describes one route's effect, not a training or recovery process. It says nothing about recovery over hours or days.
- Noticed: groups differ in age (about 33 versus 22 years), sex mix, and experience at the same time. Group differences cannot be separated from age or other factors. The "beginner" group had a mean IRCRA of 10 and could lead climb after about a month, so it is not a typical novice group.
- Noticed: the experienced group climbed harder routes at their on-sight limit. Beginners climbed easier routes. A smaller strength drop in beginners may reflect lower load, not "adaptation". The authors say this themselves.
- Noticed: reporting problems. Tables are described as medians but show means. One long-jump value (2.5 ± 3.8 m) and one RFD spread (about 10,000) look like typing errors. Table captions and body text conflict on which hand and which test each table covers. The text says the strength loss was 10% (dominant) and 7% (non-dominant), but the table values suggest the reverse. Text says almost no variable differed between groups, yet the tables mark many differences.
- Noticed: [CLAIM] The abstract mentions correlations between cortisol, anxiety, and self-confidence. I found no cortisol measurement or correlation analysis in the methods or results. Treat that statement as unsupported.
- Noticed: [CLAIM] The abstract says advanced climbers had "better sympathetic modulation" and lower anxiety. Only perceived stress and (partly) somatic anxiety differed. Cognitive anxiety, self-confidence and STAI did not. The reading of lower pre-climb RMSSD as better stress handling is debatable.
- Noticed: [CLAIM] "Best predictor of fatigue" was not tested with a predictive model or validity and reliability data. A 7-10% mean drop after one route is small, and no test-retest error for the sensor test is reported.
- Noticed: many outcomes with no stated correction for multiple testing, which raises the chance of false positives.
- Noticed: the course author is first author, and the paper promotes affordable portable force sensors for coaches. The authors declare no conflict of interest. Link to the course is not stated in the paper.
- Noticed: lead sport climbing on a rope, not bouldering. Fatigue after one long route may not match fatigue after a bouldering session.

### Practical implications for an agent

- [INFER] Weight: low for training decisions; low-to-moderate for the general idea that experienced climbers handle stress differently and show finger fatigue more clearly.
- [INFER] For a beginner-intermediate user, the paper suggests finger strength loss after a session may be small or hard to detect, so strength tests are not a reliable fatigue gauge for them. Use simpler signals: sharp drop in climbing quality, pain or tightness in fingers or forearms, session RPE, and poor performance on a repeat of a known problem.
- [INFER] A pre/post max-pull test on a 20 mm edge may become useful once the user is stronger and owns a force sensor. Until then, it adds little. If the user has no sensor, do not suggest buying one based on this paper.
- [INFER] The paper supports a general message: newer climbers report higher stress and somatic arousal when climbing near their limit. An agent can suggest easier-grade volume, repeat problems, and gradual exposure to harder attempts. The data do not prove that these steps help.
- [INFER] Do not use heart rate or heart rate variability as a training guide. The authors themselves doubt heart rate as an intensity measure, and HRV findings are inconsistent.
- [INFER] Do not cite lower-body tests, flicker fusion, or cortisol as fatigue tools for this user.
- [INFER] Suggested wording for the user: "One study found that experienced climbers lose a few percent of finger strength after a route and beginners show no clear loss. This does not give a rule for when to rest."
- [INFER] Any recovery advice (rest days, session spacing) must come from other sources. This paper measured nothing after the first minutes post-route.
- [INFER] The psychological result is the most transferable part, but it is about lead climbing with a rope, not boulder falls onto mats.

### Evidence strength rating

- [INFER] Low. Single-session cross-sectional comparison with confounded groups, inconsistent tables, unsupported abstract claims, and a population and discipline only partly matching the user.
