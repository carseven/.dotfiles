# Trail running: Kilian Jornet case study

## Source and limits

**Source.** Kilian Jornet, *The Training Characteristics of Kilian Jornet*,
14-page PDF, account through 2018. [Read the source](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1UJ78pjMwLKKKcyjHe0uvtlhgqgUYSbFu/view).

**Scope.** A first-person account of one elite athlete's training history,
trail running, ski mountaineering, technical preparation, and race strategy.
It is not a controlled study or a general training guide. His history,
professional workload, and environment limit what transfers to other athletes.

**Tags.** `[SOURCE]` is an account or claim in the PDF, not independent
verification. `[INFER]` is an application or safety judgment, not a claim
from the PDF. `[GAP]` identifies information the source does not establish.
Cite the section and PDF page when using a source claim.

## Training history and intensity

- `[SOURCE, Introduction and Create a Base, pp. 1, 5–7]` Jornet describes
  years of mountain activity and a gradual build. He reports roughly
  1,000–1,200 total training hours a year across about 400 sessions in the
  seven years before the account. These are elite workloads, not targets.
- `[SOURCE, Endurance Training, p. 8]` He reports 88–90% of endurance
  training in I1–I3, 8–10% in I4–I5, and 2% in I6–I7. He calls I1–I3
  low intensity. His table places I3 at 75–85% of VO2max.
- `[INFER]` Do not translate those percentages or zone labels into the
  five-zone Uphill Athlete model. The reported I1–I3 share does not establish
  how much training was below the aerobic threshold. Use the athlete's
  selected model and measurements instead.
- `[SOURCE, Exercise Modes and Organization of the Season, p. 9]` He
  describes running as the main sport in the warmer season and ski
  mountaineering in the colder season. He also uses other activities and
  focused blocks while retaining some work on other qualities.
- `[GAP]` His account does not establish a safe weekly increase, ideal
  intensity distribution, or minimum maintenance dose for another athlete.

## Terrain, technique, and race preparation

- `[SOURCE, Adjust Your Training to Conditions, pp. 10–11]` He prepares
  for the expected terrain, elevation, climate, duration, and other demands.
  He values familiarity with the route and conditions.
- `[SOURCE, Working on Non Physical Aspects, pp. 11–12]` He practices
  technical movement, transitions, equipment, nutrition, and race strategy.
  He sometimes combines technical and endurance practice.
- `[SOURCE, Tapering toward Important Races, pp. 10–11]` He describes a
  short race-specific preparation and his own pre-race schedule, within a
  year that often includes frequent racing.
- `[INFER]` Match terrain and skill practice to the athlete's goal, experience,
  available routes, and conditions. Use low-risk terrain and suitable
  progression. Treat his race-week schedule as history, not a taper template.
- `[GAP]` The account does not provide a validated beginner progression,
  a general taper, or quantitative nutrition and hydration guidance.

## Logging and strength

- `[SOURCE, Note and Analyze, p. 4]` He records activity, time, elevation,
  distance, and feelings, and describes optional intensity and recovery data.
- `[INFER]` A simple log can help compare planned and completed training.
- `[INFER]` Include strength alongside running. When the athlete has enough
  training time, consider allocating 1–2 hours of existing training time to
  strength. Assess the effect on running volume, recovery, and progress.

## Safety and source conflicts

- `[SOURCE, Explore Your Body Limits, pp. 12–13]` The account describes
  extreme deprivation and altitude experiments, including serious adverse
  effects. It also suggests training through pain and avoiding medication
  or supplements to observe sensations (Training Principles, p. 2).
- `[INFER]` Do not prescribe deprivation, exhaustion, dangerous exposure, or
  training through injury pain. Fuel and hydrate appropriately, stop or
  adapt when exercise causes pain, and seek professional advice for
  persistent symptoms. Do not stop prescribed medication without a clinician.
  Altitude exposure requires appropriate acclimatization and risk assessment.
- `[GAP]` This account cannot establish that fasting, deliberate dehydration,
  extreme fatigue, or rapid altitude exposure is safe or beneficial.

## Decision guide

1. Use *Training for the Uphill Athlete* for aerobic planning and threshold
   concepts; use this case study for ideas about terrain and skill specificity.
2. Compare the athlete's goal with their recent sustainable workload,
   experience, available time, and terrain. Keep the case study's elite
   volume and zone system separate from the athlete's plan.
3. If planning a race, consider ascent, descent, surface, conditions,
   equipment, and fueling practice. Progress within the athlete's experience
   and recovery capacity.
4. Cite the case study as one athlete's experience, label adaptations as
   inference, and apply the safety rules above.

## Questions for the athlete

- What is the trail-running goal and, if relevant, its date and terrain?
- What recent running, climbing, strength, and other training is sustainable?
- What trails, elevation, time, equipment, and recovery capacity are available?
- What relevant injuries, symptoms, or health restrictions affect training?
