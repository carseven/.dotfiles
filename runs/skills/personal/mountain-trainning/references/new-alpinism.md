# Training for the New Alpinism: extended agent summary

## 1. Identity, scope, and how to use this file

- **Book:** *Training for the New Alpinism: A Manual for the Climber as Athlete*.
- **Authors:** Steve House (alpinist) and Scott Johnston (endurance coach, ex-World Cup ski coach).
- **Publication:** Patagonia Books, first edition, 2014. Illustrations by Erik Brooks.
- **Local source:** `../Training for the New Alpinism · A Manual for the Climber as Athlete-to-markdown.md` (about 7,600 lines). Line numbers below ("L1234") point into that file, so a reader can verify a claim. They are approximate (about plus or minus 30 lines).
- **Scope of the book:** endurance and strength training for alpine climbing, from mountaineering (for example Rainier) to technical alpine routes and Himalayan peaks. It also covers nutrition, altitude, mental fitness, and "training by climbing". It does not teach rock-climbing or sport-climbing finger training.
- **Core thesis:** build a very large aerobic base, add max strength, convert strength to muscular endurance, then train specifically, then taper. Do this once a year, aimed at one goal date.

### Tags used in this file

- **[B]** = stated by the book (paraphrased). Numbers marked [B] come from the book.
- **[I]** = my inference or synthesis. Treat it as a suggestion and not as book content.
- **[B?]** = the book says it, but the conversion is unclear or a figure is missing.

### How to use this file

1. Read section 8 (decision guide) and section 9 (questions) first when planning for an athlete.
2. Use sections 3 to 7 to justify a recommendation and to find numbers.
3. Use section 10 before quoting a number. Some tables and charts are missing from the conversion.
4. Never present the book's sample plans as a prescription. The book itself says plans are an outline and the training log is the real record [B, L2650].
5. This file paraphrases the book. For exact wording, open the source file at the cited lines.
6. Health caveat [B, disclaimer L7531+]: the book tells readers to get medical clearance. Repeat this to athletes with health risks.

## 3. Core principles

### 3.1 Two types of training (general vs specific)

- **Idea [B, L682]:** all training is either (1) general conditioning that supports later work, or (2) specific work that models the event. General work is a base of support. It need not help climbing directly, except for low-fitness athletes.
- **Numbers [B, L489]:**
  - Specific training can be under 10% of total volume for young people and beginners. It reaches about 30% for world-class athletes.
  - The authors describe the best ski racers as spending about 80% of volume on non-event-specific work.
- **Apply:**
  - Break the goal route into components (aerobic pace, loaded vertical, technical skill, grip and upper-body endurance, cold, altitude). Train each component, not the whole route, most of the year.
  - Use whole-route simulations late in the plan.
- **Caveat:**
  - Experienced climbers with decades of base can use climbing as most of their training [B, L1010].
  - Beginners cannot copy that approach [B].
- **Source:** Ch 1, Ch 2 "The Two Types of Training".

### 3.2 Training effect and supercompensation

- **Idea [B, L734]:** stress plus recovery causes adaptation. Only systems that are stressed adapt. The body can end above its starting level, but only if load is progressive and recovery is enough. Too little stress gives no response. Too much blocks adaptation.
- **Apply:** judge training by the body's response, not by the work done. Plan recovery as part of the stimulus.
- **Caveat:** different systems adapt at different speeds. Enzymes and mitochondria respond in hours to days. Capillaries and connective tissue take weeks to months or years [B, L1700 region].
- **Source:** Ch 2 "The Training Effect".

### 3.3 Continuity

- **Idea [B, L760]:** regular training with few interruptions. Without it, gradualness and modulation do not help [B, L2640].
- **Apply:**
  - Occasional gaps of a few days are fine if they cut monthly load by about 5% or less.
  - Gaps above about 10% of monthly volume need an adjusted plan [B].
  - Do not pretend a missed week happened. Re-enter at the load the body is adapted to.
- **Illness rule [B, L1260]:** stop at first signs of illness. Never train with fever. After illness, give one easy day for each day lost.
- **Caveat:** do not use continuity as a reason to train when sick or injured. The book says the opposite.
- **Source:** Ch 2 "Continuity", "Returning to Training after a Break".

### 3.4 Gradualness

- **Idea [B, L770]:** the body can only absorb small increases in load.
- **Numbers [B]:**
  - Beginners: up to about 25% more volume per year and up to about 50% within a three-week cycle.
  - Advanced athletes: about 10% per year at most.
  - Elite athletes: often no volume increase. They change the mix of intensity and specificity.
  - Climb-to-train weekly rule: do not raise volume more than 5 to 10% per week [B, L6660].
- **Apply:** use a small weekly step, then a recovery week (see 5.3 for the book's own patterns).
- **Caveat:** after a long layoff, muscles return faster than tendons. Feeling strong early is a risk signal [B, L2760].
- **Source:** Ch 2, Ch 7.

### 3.5 Modulation

- **Idea [B, L776]:** alternate harder and easier loading (easy/hard, or easy/medium/hard). It applies from hours to months.
- **Numbers [B]:**
  - Adaptation runs in roughly 3-to-6-week cycles. The first three weeks adapt quickly. The next three give less if load stays constant.
  - After 3 to 4 weeks of build-up, cut volume and intensity by about half [B, L6665].
- **Apply:**
  - Raise load after a plateau, not during it.
  - An experienced athlete may modulate by feel and use short planned over-reaching, then recovery (House note, L3440).
- **Caveat:** the "by feel" method needs experience and body awareness. Beginners should keep a fixed pattern.
- **Source:** Ch 2, Ch 8 (side note on modulating load).

### 3.6 Specificity

- **Idea [B, L781]:** only the stressed systems and fibres adapt. Beginners gain from any aerobic work. As fitness grows, gains need work that matches the event.
- **Apply:**
  - Prefer weight-bearing uphill work (hiking, running, ski touring) over cycling or swimming.
  - Use cycling and swimming as recovery or variety for fit athletes.
  - Train in the boots, pack weight, and terrain of the goal when possible.
- **Caveat:** the book calls cycling efficient and therefore a weaker training tool per hour. It still counts as general aerobic base for the unfit [B].
- **Source:** Ch 2 "Specificity".

### 3.7 Individualisation

- **Idea [B, L803]:** people respond differently to the same plan. Coach Canova is paraphrased: the same input gives each athlete a different response.
- **Apply:**
  - Treat any spreadsheet plan as a draft.
  - Adjust to age, history, stress, time budget, health, and the log.
  - Decide a hard session on the day, after a warm-up [B, L3390].
- **Caveat:** the book warns against detailed day-by-day plans set months ahead. It suggests planning detail only two to three weeks ahead [B, L3385].
- **Source:** Ch 2 "Individuality", Ch 8.

### 3.8 Fatigue and recovery

- **Idea [B, L1041]:** training weakens, recovery strengthens. Recovery is trainable too (Twight essay, L1500).
- **Recovery time rules of thumb [B, L1240]:**
  - High-intensity work and strength training: two to three full days before the next similar load.
  - Low-intensity endurance of up to two hours: about 8 to 24 hours.
  - Long sessions above two hours, even easy: about 24 to 48 hours (glycogen).
- **Tools [B]:** food within 30 to 60 minutes after long sessions, sleep (REM and growth hormone), easy recovery sessions, massage, cold or contrast baths, compression, electro-stimulation. The book treats these as aids, not substitutes for sleep and food.
- **Base-period fatigue [B, L1020]:** mild constant tiredness is expected in the base period. Do not expect personal bests.
- **Caveat:** the book gives anecdotal support for some tools. Treat them as optional [I].
- **Source:** Ch 2 "Fatigue and Recovery".

### 3.9 Overtraining and warning signs

- **Idea [B, L1289]:** short-term soreness or tiredness is "over-reaching" and is normal. Chronic over-reaching that stops adaptation is overtraining. It can cost months and shorten careers. Better to be slightly undertrained.
- **Signs the book lists:**
  - Poor motivation, especially after a good warm-up.
  - Exhaustion or deep soreness lasting a day or two after a session.
  - No week-to-week progress in energy, strength, or fitness.
  - Repeated poor session grades (see 4.5).
  - Stagnation or decline despite consistent work.
  - Recurring small injuries or tendon problems, which show that adaptation is not happening [B, L1413].
- **What to feel [B, L1420]:** a little tired right after a session (often energised), good the next day, and keen for the next session.
- **Apply:** when signs appear, cut load, rest, review sleep, food, stress, and recovery-session intensity. Act early.
- **Caveat:** resting heart rate alone is a weak signal (see 4.5).
- **Source:** Ch 2 "Overtraining", "What Should You Feel?".

## 4. Energy systems, intensity, testing, load tracking

### 4.1 Aerobic base and why it dominates

- **Idea [B, L1561]:** the aerobic base is the muscles' ability to make energy aerobically plus the heart's ability to deliver blood. Alpine days last many hours, so endurance is mainly limited by aerobic metabolism.
- **Three muscle adaptations [B, L1710]:** more mitochondrial mass, more aerobic enzymes, denser capillaries. Duration and frequency of low-intensity work are the best stimulus. Intensity matters less.
- **Further items [B, L1781]:** heart pump volume, blood oxygen capacity, muscle oxygen storage.
- **Aerobic threshold (AeT) [B, L880]:** the single most important measure of the aerobic system for climbers. Raising the pace at AeT is the main goal of base training.
- **VO2 max view [B, L1565]:** VO2 max sets potential, but the fraction you can sustain matters more for long events. VO2 max responds poorly to training in experienced athletes. The authors tell a story of a skier whose VO2 max did not move over five years of focused work.
- **Fuel [B, L1635]:** fat is the large store. Sugar stores are small (about 2,000 kcal, an hour or so of harder work). Aerobic training shifts the muscle to use fat at higher intensities.
- **Lactate shuttle [B, L2013]:** fast fibres make lactate. Well-trained slow fibres use it as fuel. This is why a big aerobic base supports high-power efforts.
- **Fibres [B, L1900]:** the body recruits slow fibres first. Fast fibres join when force or pace rises. Only recruited fibres adapt.
- **Aerobic deficiency [B, L876]:** some athletes cannot keep heart rate low even at slow speeds. The book says there is a cure through patient low-intensity work. It points readers to Twight's essay (L1463) and Ch 3.
- **Apply:** most volume goes into Zone 1. Do not replace duration with intensity.
- **Caveat:** the book's authors lean on the "muscle power" model and on coaching experience. They cite mixed-quality studies [I].

### 4.2 Heart-rate zones as the book defines them [B, L860 to L945]

Zones use percent of maximum heart rate (HR). Ranges are the book's, and they vary by person.

| Zone | Book's label | % max HR (book) | How it should feel | Book's use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recovery | recovery | below 55 | Very easy. Should leave you fresher. | Recovery only. Not training. |
| 1 | basic endurance | 55 to 75 (up to 85 in very well-trained) | Conversational. Nose breathing is quiet. | Bulk of all training. The key to the aerobic base. |
| 2 | "no man's land" | 75 to 80 | Feels like training, but is hard for little gain in the unfit. | Use sparingly unless the athlete has a large base. In elite athletes it merges into Zone 1. |
| 3 | upper aerobic | 80 to 90 (top near 92 to 94 in elite) | "Fun hard". Can talk in a few words. Could hold about an hour. Another gear is still available. | Main hard aerobic work on a base. Overuse hurts the base. |
| 4 | anaerobic / VO2max | 90 to 95 | Few words. Strong anaerobic share. | Small share even for the best (about 2 to 6% of yearly volume for elite endurance athletes). Low value for alpine events. |
| 5 | maximum | not HR-guided | All-out for roughly 10 to 15 seconds. | Builds strength and power (hill sprints, crux moves). |

- **Use the full range of a zone.** Do not push to the top of each range every time [B].
- **Perceived effort is unreliable [B].** The book recommends a heart-rate monitor for at least some training.
- **Ventilation markers [B]:**
  - Upper limit of Zone 1 = where nose breathing becomes noisy or laboured (corresponds to AeT).
  - Upper limit of Zone 3 = "break-away breathing" (corresponds to the anaerobic or lactate threshold).
- **"Low intensity" is not slow in a fit athlete.** The book prefers "long easy distance" to "long slow distance" [B, L890].
- **Zone 4 gate [B, L930]:** top coaches delay Zone 4 until the pace at AeT is within about 10% (5% for world class) of the pace at the anaerobic threshold. Zone 4 and intervals are described as nearly irrelevant for climbs lasting 6 to 8 hours or more.
- **Apply [I]:** when an athlete has no monitor, use nose-breathing and "can speak full sentences" as Zone 1 limits.
- **Caveat:** percent-of-max zones are an approximation. They depend on a true max HR and on training history.

### 4.3 Testing: max heart rate and field tests

- **Max HR [B, L975]:**
  - Do not use "220 minus age". The book says population formulas can be far off for individuals.
  - Field test: warm up about 15 minutes, with the last minutes moderate and not tiring. Then run hard for two minutes uphill (about 6 to 10% grade). The last 20 seconds should be all-out. Read HR at the end. The book says it should be within about five beats of max.
  - Preconditions: healthy, rested, and motivated. Fatigue is the usual reason for a low result.
  - Cycling usually cannot reach true max unless standing, or if the athlete is well trained.
  - [I] Advise medical clearance before a max test for older or at-risk athletes. The book says "not for the faint of heart".
- **Lab and device options [B]:** blood lactate meter (costly), lab treadmill step test, ventilation reading by feel.
- **Alpine Combine and box step test [B, L2539]:**
  - Timed 1,000 ft (305 m) of vertical gain on steep class 2 or 3 terrain with a pack of about 20% of body weight. Fallback: timed box steps with the same pack, in boots, to reach the same vertical. Box height about 75% of shin length (typically 8 to 12 inches).
  - Plus counts in 60 seconds of dips, sit-ups, pull-ups, box jumps, push-ups.
  - Purpose: a repeatable general-fitness baseline, retested to see progress. Athletes may invent their own test.
  - **Scoring tables and benchmark values are not present in the conversion** (see section 10).
  - The book advises against stair machines for this test because the step falls away [B].
- **Goal-specific tests [B, L3790 region]:** the Makalu 2011 example shows using a day on the real mountain as a test, and turning back when pace falls short.

### 4.4 Measuring and tracking load

- **Hours in zones are the main currency [B, L2660].** Steve's plan counts hours in Zones 1 and 2 (HR range 120 to 160 for him) and includes strength time.
- **Annual volume reference [B, L2688]:** the book offers rough yearly hours: about 250 for an active college climber, 200 for a working professional, 400 for a seasonal worker, 500 for a working guide. The authors call these guesses.
- **Elite context [B, L704]:** top endurance athletes train about 16 hours a week (runners) to 25 to 30 (cyclists). House trained 820 to 942 hours per year from 2003 to 2009. The authors state this is a yardstick, not a target.
- **Starting volume rule [B, L2670]:** start the transition at about 50% of last year's average weekly hours. If there is no history, guess.
- **Intensity distribution [B]:**
  - Top endurance athletes spend more than three-quarters of volume in Zone 1 (L915).
  - A coach quote says about 90% of a World Cup skier's training time is Zone 1 to 2 (L1530).
  - 800 m runners in base training run about 90% of miles below lactate threshold (L1030).
  - Zone 4 is about 2 to 6% of elite yearly volume (L925).
- **Training log [B, L2645]:**
  - Record training, how it felt, and recovery. Add a grade per session. Review trends.
  - Use a simple notebook, calendar, or spreadsheet. The authors host templates at stevehouse.net/downloads.html [B?].
  - Do not let the numbers drive training or turn the log into a competition [B].
  - Plan loosely two to three weeks ahead, with a quarterly volume template.
- **Apply [I]:** track weekly hours by zone, vertical gain, loaded vertical, strength sessions, sleep, and the session grade.

### 4.5 Monitoring tools [B, L1173]

- **Session grades:** A to F scale from "felt superhuman" to "could not train". More than two Cs in a row, or a C and a D within two days, means stop and assess.
- **Morning resting HR:** an elevation of more than about 10% for a couple of days is a flag. The authors found the method unreliable alone.
- **HRV:** the authors rate it as the best practical monitor, with a short orthostatic-type test and consumer devices. They say it is best used with a subjective assessment.
- **Caveat [I]:** the book dates from 2014. Current wearables differ, and their algorithms were not evaluated by the authors.

## 5. Periodisation

### 5.1 General logic

- **Idea [B, L981]:** break the year into periods, each with a focus. Each supports the next. The more advanced the athlete and the higher the goal, the more periodisation matters.
- **Order [B]:** Recovery, Transition, Base, Specific, then Taper and the goal. Overlap is allowed.
- **Goal date [B, L2561]:**
  - Plan around one-year cycles. Near-peak fitness two or three times a year needs excellent coaching. A self-coached athlete gets one peak of about 4 to 8 weeks.
  - A trip fewer than 12 weeks away means an abbreviated cycle and lower gains.
  - Long-term goals (five to ten years, or more) are encouraged.
- **Work backwards [I]:** set the goal date, subtract the taper, specific period, base (including strength blocks), and transition. If the sum exceeds the time available, shorten the transition first and the specific period second. Protect the base.

### 5.2 Period summary

| Period | Purpose [B] | Duration logic [B] | Entry / exit [B unless marked] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recovery | Recover after a big climb or season, including mentally. | At least 1 to 3 weeks off hard climbing. Longer after big efforts (L1000). | Exit when tired-brain and body signs are gone [I]. |
| Transition | Become an athlete again. General aerobic work, general strength, easy climbing. Learn how the body responds. | 8 weeks for most. 2 to 3 weeks for seasoned athletes, 4 to 6 weeks for a fit athlete after a short break. Beginners 8 weeks (L1040). | Enter at about 50% of last year's weekly hours. Exit after a lighter week 8. |
| Base | Raise general resistance to fatigue and the aerobic threshold. "Put money in the bank." | At least 12 weeks for most. 8 to 10 for very fit with years of training. 16 to 24 weeks for low background or expeditions (L1070). | Exit when the base can support specific work. A minimum of 12 good base weeks before specific training. |
| Max strength (inside base) | Raise strength reserve through neural adaptation. | About 8 weeks (L3240). | Follows transition strength. Then maintain. |
| Conversion to muscular endurance (inside base) | Turn strength into climbing-specific fatigue resistance. | Starts after max strength. Runs through base weeks 9 to 20 in the ch 8 template. | Needs a strength base and an aerobic base. |
| Specific | Combine qualities in goal-like workouts. | A few weeks (inexperienced) to about two months (experienced). The book's template is 4 weeks. | Skip if base is under 12 weeks or the timeline is 4 months or less (L3690). |
| Taper and peak | Let supercompensation show. | About 2 weeks after good training. 4 to 7 days if training was under 4 months. Peak lasts up to about 6 weeks with maintenance. | Reduce volume and intensity. Keep light aerobic work. |

### 5.3 Transition period details [B, Ch 7]

- Load pattern for the eight weeks [B, L2711 table]: weeks 1 to 3 flat at 50% of last year's average, weeks 4 to 5 about 10% more, weeks 6 to 7 about 5% more, week 8 cut by about 50%.
- Sessions [B]: one long Zone 1 workout (about 25% of weekly aerobic volume), one Zone 2 workout (cap about 10 to 15% of aerobic volume), two general strength sessions, one climbing day of 5 to 8 pitches 1 to 2 grades below limit, easy volume to fill the rest.
- Steve's 2009 Makalu transition: six weeks, 8.5 to 11 hours, 5% weekly steps. It is described as the shortest the authors ever recommend [B, L2660].
- Goals for strength: general strength, flexibility, coordination [B, L2855].

### 5.4 Base period template [B, Ch 8, L3470 to L3640]

The chart images are missing. The text gives the following frame for mountaineering and technical objectives.

- **Weeks 1 to 8:**
  - Start about 10% above the last high week of transition.
  - Weeks 1 to 3: +10 to 20% per week.
  - Week 4: -25 to 35%.
  - Weeks 5 to 6: hold at the week 3 level.
  - Week 7: +25%, with Zone 3 time halved and strength capped at about three hours.
  - Week 8: -50%.
  - Content: max strength twice a week with the core routine as warm-up, one long Zone 1 day (25 to 30% of volume), one Zone 1 to 2 steep day (about 20%), Zone 3 in weeks 5 to 7, easy volume to fill.
  - Technical athletes add one day of technical climbing and one cragging day two grades below onsight limit.
- **Weeks 9 to 16:**
  - Weeks 9 to 11: +5 to 10% per week.
  - Week 12: -50%.
  - Weeks 13 to 14: hold.
  - Week 15: +20% overload.
  - Week 16: -50 to 70%.
  - Content: muscular endurance 1 to 2 times a week, abbreviated max strength as warm-up, two long Zone 1 sessions (30% and 20%), one Zone 3 hill session (about 10%).
  - Pack carrying starts at about 10% of body weight on the long days, +5% every other week, up to a maximum of 30%.
  - Technical athletes add two days of moderate alpine climbing and one midweek climbing session.
- **Weeks 17 to 20:**
  - Week 17: same as week 15.
  - Week 18: -25%.
  - Weeks 19 to 20: same as week 17.
  - Content: one max strength plus one muscular endurance session in even weeks, muscular endurance only in odd weeks. Muscular endurance is done in Zone 3 and is very tiring. Everything else easy.
  - Technical athletes drop cragging and add climbing-specific muscular endurance.
- **If only 8 weeks are available [B, L6640 region]:** do only the max strength sessions plus the volume. Muscular endurance then comes from the trip itself.
- **Caveat:** the book says these are the authors' templates. Adjust by response [B].

### 5.5 Specific period templates [B, Ch 9]

- **Principles [B, L3700]:**
  - "Training to train": the base exists to allow this work.
  - Almost all other training becomes recovery or maintenance.
  - Non-weight-bearing aerobic work is now only for recovery.
  - If it does not need a big act of will, it may be too easy.
- **Trip-based version [B, L3715]:** House's pre-Nanga Parbat trip to Peru, with each climb harder, longer, or higher than the last and 1 to 4 days of rest between. A test of the facsimile can lead to the decision not to go (Makalu 2011).
- **Home-based mountaineering template [B, L3980], four weeks:**
  - Volume 80%, 75%, 70%, 70% of base week 20.
  - One "big day" at 100% of the goal's biggest vertical with half the load (week 1), then 150% over two back-to-back days at full load (weeks 3 and 4).
  - One sustained Zone 3 uphill session in boots at 50% rising to 80 to 90% of the biggest day's vertical, carrying 75% rising to 100% of the planned load.
  - One Zone 1 to 2 midweek session, one max strength maintenance session, all else recovery.
- **Home-based technical template [B, L4020], four weeks:**
  - Two big days a week (at least 72 hours apart), each with 100% then 110% then 120% of the biggest single day's vertical and technical climbing. Packs at about 50% of expected weight.
  - One muscular endurance or limit-cragging day.
  - Alternative week 4: a "graduation" road-trip climb on a goal-like peak.
- **Scott's K2 example [B, L3750]:** three key workouts a week (steep water-jug hill laps, a long all-day ski tour, an after-work loaded ski ascent) with easy days between. The authors say that only a person with Scott's base could copy it.
- **Caveat:** do not use the specific period without the base. The book says "done improperly, this will bury you" [B, L4010].

### 5.6 Taper and peak [B, Ch 10]

- **Purpose:** rest to let supercompensation appear. Cut volume and intensity sharply. Keep one or two easy Zone 1 to 2 outings a week.
- **Why keep some aerobic work:** aerobic capacity fades first. The book cites a large mitochondrial loss after about five days of bed rest. It adds that light work keeps the system "on duty".
- **Examples [B]:** Scott's taper was the travel and trek to K2 (volume about -50%). House took four days off, a beach week, three weeks of maintenance, then a week of rest during travel before Nanga Parbat.
- **Prolonging a peak [B, L4150]:** after about six weeks, do two to three weeks of base and strength work to build a second peak. If stagnating on a project, go back to base work for two weeks.
- **Trek is not training [B, L4170]:** do not try to get fit on the approach. Go easy, use conversational pace, avoid illness.
- **Jet lag [B, L4180]:** about a day per time-zone hour to feel normal. Shift meals and sleep to destination time. The book has direction-specific light advice (see the book's table).

## 6. Strength training

### 6.1 Why strength matters for endurance athletes

- **Idea [B, L2085]:** a larger "strength reserve" lowers the share of maximum strength needed for a task, so endurance rises. The book cites Helgerud's studies where a short block of max-strength work (about nine weeks, 30 minutes a week) raised ski-specific time to exhaustion strongly [B].
- **Mechanism [B, L2107]:** neural. More motor units become reliably available. Fresh units replace tired ones. "Muscles that wire together, fire together".
- **Examples [B]:** a 60 lb Denali pack and a 12-inch step; an ice climber who lost grip at the crux without feeling pumped and fixed it with max-grip work.
- **Second reason [B, L2250]:** stronger athletes get fewer injuries, which matters with a heavy pack and awkward positions.
- **Power vs endurance trade-off [B, L2125]:** more power can mean less endurance. A very high peak in both is impossible. Alpinists need a mix. The piano-mover story warns against carrying extra muscle mass.

### 6.2 Three kinds of strength work in the book

| Type | Purpose | Prescription (book) | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| General strength | Broad base, coordination, flexibility, reduce injury risk | 50 to 60% of max load. Circuit style. Alternate upper and lower body. Add one lap every two weeks (1 up to 4). 30 seconds between exercises, 3 minutes between laps. Twice a week. Avoid failure [B, L2990]. | Transition. About 6 to 8 weeks for beginners, as short as 4 for experienced. |
| Max strength | Raise strength reserve by neural gains | 2 to 4 exercises. Load near 85 to 90% of one-rep max (about 3 to 5 reps possible). Sets of 1 to 4 reps. 4 to 6 sets. 3 to 5 minutes rest. Do not go to failure. Twice a week [B, L3140]. | About 8 weeks. Then maintain. |
| Muscular endurance (ME) | Convert strength into fatigue resistance in climbing muscles | Heavier load with fewer reps for the experienced. Lighter load with many reps for beginners (examples range from about 15 to 100 reps per set). Rest 2 to 5 minutes for heavy sets and 30 to 60 seconds for light sets [B, L2410]. | Conversion period and base weeks 9 to 20. |

- **Hypertrophy block [B, L2395]:** the book skips it for most alpinists because added mass must be carried uphill. It suggests it for very light, adolescent, or very weak athletes.
- **Max-strength maintenance [B, L3245]:** after the block, a short session every 10 to 14 days (or as a warm-up for ME) keeps most gains. Without it, strength reserve fades.
- **Plateau fixes [B, L2440]:** drop volume for a session or two, check recovery, or use isometric holds (3 to 5 holds of 5 seconds at several joint angles).
- **Women [B, L2277]:** the book says max-strength work gives large neural gains without bulk. Packs weigh the same for everyone, so strength-to-weight matters.

### 6.3 Exercise selection logic

- **Selection logic [B, L2970]:** pick big, whole-body movements first. Then narrow to two to four exercises that match the athlete's weaknesses and the goal. The book states there is nothing magic about its exercise list. Follow the principles if equipment differs.
- **Examples named [B]:**
  - Legs: box step-ups (the book calls this essential), split squats, hill sprints, tire drag, car or sled push.
  - Upper body: pull-ups with variations (offsets, lock-offs, other grips), one-arm isometric tool hangs, overhead ladder dynos, treadmill hand-walking for ME.
  - Whole body and core: Turkish get-up, push-up variations, a core routine (strict sit-ups, hip-mobility work, rotational and anti-rotation holds).
- **Pull-up programme [B, L3160]:** a simple eight-week max-strength plan with added weight so only a few reps are possible. The book reports gains without body-weight gain.
- **Caution [B, L3030]:** large volumes of plain fixed-bar pull-ups can raise the risk of elbow tendon problems.
- **Hill sprints [B, L3199]:** 8 to 10 seconds, about 6 to 8 reps, long rests, steep grade (10% minimum, 20 to 50% better). They train power and some max strength. Once every two weeks after the block is a maintenance dose.

### 6.4 Core

- **Idea [B, L2337]:** climbing is quadrupedal. The core links hips and shoulders so limb force reaches the terrain. A pack makes terrain feel steeper.
- **Method [B, L2880]:** use strict form, hold until form fails, and do not hold the breath. Use the routine as a warm-up for strength days, and as a stand-alone session two or three times through. Maintain at about once every two weeks in later base.
- **Caution [B]:** sit-ups and crunches mostly train the strongest abdominal muscle. High repetitions train endurance more than strength. Poor form defeats the goal.

### 6.5 Upper body for technical goals

- **Idea [B, L3320]:** technical alpine goals need upper-body max strength and ME. Mountaineering-only goals need mostly legs.
- **ME options [B]:** long alpine or ice routes, gym laps in boots with a pack (start with about 10% body weight, cap about 20% unless finger strength is strong), rotating walls, treadmill hand-walking.
- **Caution [B]:** high-volume weighted climbing raises tendon risk and skin wear.

### 6.6 Interference and scheduling

- **Idea [B, L2347]:** energy is limited. Heavy climbing and heavy gym sessions compete. Periodisation separates them.
- **Apply:**
  - Keep hard strength and hard endurance on separate days or separate blocks.
  - Max-strength sessions can follow tiring sessions as recovery-friendly work if done correctly (L2380).
  - Allow 72 hours between hard loaded-climb sessions [B].
- **Caveat:** the book states climbers resist backing off hard climbing in base. It says this reduces absolute climbing level for a while [B, L2360].
- **Injury prevention [B]:**
  - Gradual progress, a low-load early block, circuit style to limit soreness.
  - Strong core.
  - Caution after layoff (tendons lag).
  - Tony Yaniro's sidebar: tissues after injury tolerate far less load, so avoid injury.

## 7. Mountain-specific topics

### 7.1 Climbing fitness and what to train by goal type

- **Mountaineering (for example Rainier) [B, L520]:** training is mostly aerobic. Hiking and running in the mountains plus strength. Add snow and glacier peaks later. Technique matters less.
- **Technical alpine [B, L3470, L6440]:**
  - Same aerobic base, plus upper-body strength and ME, plus skills.
  - If skill already exceeds the route's technical level, put the time in the base and not in harder crag climbing. Most alpine routes are climbed below a climber's crag limit [B].
  - If skill is below the route, develop skills first or lower the goal.
- **Speed is safety [B, L650].**
- **Moderate alpine mileage [B, L6633, Colin Haley]:** time on easy and moderate alpine terrain builds fitness, efficiency, and mental ease on poorly protected ground.

### 7.2 Technique and skills

- **Idea [B, L6405]:** "going climbing" is practice. "Training by climbing" applies continuity, gradualness, and modulation to a climbing year.
- **Skill goals [B, L6420]:** House broke goals into components: lead grades, speed on uphill, a standard for carrying a pack uphill, bad-condition climbing, mountain-craft skills (avalanche, navigation, weather, ropework).
- **Soviet example [B, L1990]:** about 40 to 100 simpler climbs before an advanced ascent, with rope work drilled until automatic. This is a sidebar about the old Soviet system.
- **Apply [I]:** for technical goals, keep a separate skills ledger (rope work, protection, descent, navigation, bivouac, avalanche, rescue) from the fitness plan. The book treats the skills as essential and says no book can teach them.
- **Boundary:** this book gives little technique instruction. See `../the-climbing-bible.md` for climbing movement and finger-strength work (see `synthesis.md`).

### 7.3 Pack carrying and loaded uphill

- **Water-jug carries [B, L3265]:** the book's preferred ME method for legs. Steep, long hill, boots, a pack of water that is poured out at the top.
  - Regulate by breathing. Stay at the top of conversational pace at first. Add Zone 3 later.
  - Leg-limited, not breath-limited. About 20 to 25% of body weight for conditioned legs. About 10% for the least conditioned.
  - Start at about 50% of the goal day's vertical and 0 to 50% of the goal load. End at or above the goal's biggest day.
  - Progress over 8 to 12 weeks after max strength, and finish about two weeks before the objective. Allow 72 hours of recovery after each demanding session [B, L3330].
- **Base-period carrying [B]:** start at 10% of body weight on long days, add 5% every other week, do not exceed 30%.
- **Substitutes [B]:** stadium stairs, high-rise stairs, a 12-inch box, rocks in a pack.
- **Examples [B]:** a 58-year-old novice for Rainier's Disappointment Cleaver used Zone 1 loaded uphill as her core workouts. The progression tables are partly missing.

### 7.4 Altitude

- **Physiology [B, L4971]:** the muscle needs the same oxygen at any altitude, but the ability to supply it falls. The book names two routes to acclimatise. Oxygen delivery rises as a natural response to going high (ventilation, red cells, pH change). Muscle efficiency in using oxygen only changes through earlier training.
- **Acclimatisation strategies [B, L5179]:**
  - "Climb high, sleep low", with average sleeping altitude gain of about 300 m (1,000 ft) per day (Houston's rule).
  - Soviet system: sleep high for a night, then descend to rest for a similar number of days. A trip is two to four days, rest the same length.
  - Scott/Prezelj method: multiple peaks to build acclimatisation.
  - Denali method: climb an easy route first, for example the West Buttress before a technical Denali route.
- **Typical pattern [B, L5230]:** about 10 days at 4,300 to 5,300 m, then quick ascents to about 7,000 m, for sub-7,000 m goals in about three weeks.
- **Pre-acclimatisation [B, L5337]:** the authors found no evidence that hypoxic tents or similar tools help for high mountains. Living near big mountains and going high often helps.
- **De-acclimatisation [B, L5530]:** the rule of thumb is that adaptation is lost at about the rate it is gained. Studies differ.
- **Weight and eating [B, L5419]:** the authors oppose gaining fat before an expedition. Eat often, mostly carbohydrate, while acclimatising.
- **Illness [B, L5531]:** AMS, HACE, and HAPE exist. The book's first treatment is descent. It lists several drugs and cites other books for detail (Hackett, Houston, Farris). **This file gives no medication guidance.** Send athletes to a physician with altitude experience [I].
- **Sleep [B]:** poor sleep is expected at new altitudes. Trouble falling asleep after hard work can mean overstress.
- **Taper link [B]:** keep heart rate low at altitude, using breathing as a governor.

### 7.5 Nutrition and fuelling

- **Macros [B, L4228]:**
  - Carbohydrate: the book warns against too little. Minimum about 5 g/kg/day, with 7 to 10 g/kg at very high expenditure. An Everest study found about 3 g/kg.
  - Protein: 1.2 to 1.4 g/kg/day for endurance athletes. Up to 2.0 g/kg for ultra-endurance or heavy volume. The book does not support high-protein, low-carbohydrate diets.
  - Fat: needed. Nine kcal/g.
- **Training days [B, L4348]:**
  - Eat a full breakfast two to four hours before training.
  - Eat during sessions over about two hours.
  - For efforts over an hour, sport-science guidance is 30 to 70 g of carbohydrate per hour. The authors admit climbers rarely can eat that much.
  - The authors experimented with fasted easy sessions under two hours. They say this is outside standard recommendations.
- **After training [B, L4464]:** carbohydrate, protein, fluid, and electrolytes within 30 to 60 minutes. Repeat carbohydrate about every two hours for up to six hours.
- **On the route [B, L4540]:**
  - Big meals are hard at an alpine start. Treat the first 60 to 90 minutes as a warm-up at conversational pace.
  - On long, low-intensity days eat small, frequent, balanced snacks.
  - At high intensity or very high altitude, favour carbohydrate (gels at least about one per hour, up to about three).
  - For efforts over five to six hours, replace at least about half of the calories burned. The ideal 70 g/hour is usually not possible.
  - The Slovak Direct example shows bonking after stopping to eat.
- **Hydration [B, L4647]:** many people overdrink. The book cites "drink to thirst" with water (Noakes) and the more traditional ACSM limits (avoid more than about 2% body-weight loss). Sweating at altitude is wasteful. Dehydration is higher at altitude.
- **Rule [B]:** practise the food and drink plan in training.
- **Not covered here [I]:** vitamin, mineral, and supplement sections (L4793) were not read in detail.

### 7.6 Mental

- **Overview [B, L5739]:** the authors present motivation, emotion, fear, fulfilment, concentration, flow, confidence, and transcendence. They say this chapter is less evidence-based than the rest.
- **Points to use [B]:**
  - Motivation cannot be coached. Training is also mental training.
  - Concentration can be practised during climbing. Confidence comes from preparation and repeated practice.
  - Training the "weak" qualities improves total package (Borysewicz quote).
  - Moulin's sidebar: rest is the foundation, and mental management is a major share of coaching.
- **Apply [I]:** include simulation days (cold, dark, loaded, long) to rehearse suffering and decisions.

### 7.7 Recovery and tapering

See 3.8 and 5.6. Also [B, L6721]: after a big trip or expedition, take real time off from climbing. The authors warn that climbers who are always out take more risk and that "you can't live there all the time".

### 7.8 Time budget, working life, and long-term planning

- **Yearly hours [B]:** see 4.4 for the book's rough annual hours. Lower hours are a "reasonable starting place" and the point is progress [B, L720].
- **Weekend-warrior structure [B, L3475]:** the base template assumes a job or school and free weekends. It places the long days on the weekend. Full-time climbers can replace generic work with real climbing.
- **Low climbing days per month [B, L1025]:** athletes who do long climbing days fewer than six or seven times per month need the base especially.
- **Short timelines [B]:**
  - Under 12 weeks: abbreviate.
  - 4 months or less: skip the specific period and extend the base.
  - 8 weeks of base: do max strength plus volume, and let the trip supply ME.
- **Planned breaks [B, L1280]:** build load for a week before a planned break, then treat the break as recovery.
- **Time-poor trap [B, L1515, Twight]:** attempts to replace volume with short, hard sessions fail for long events. Coach Johnston's experience was that time-crunched athletes did better with a Zone 1 to 2 base plus timed Zone 3 to 4 work.
- **Plan detail horizon [B]:** plan two to three weeks ahead and decide the daily session after warming up.
- **Social note [B, L6850]:** training for alpinism can be lonely. Recruit partners.

### 7.9 Age, sex, and experience adaptations

- **Experience [B]:**
  - Gradual rate depends on years of training.
  - Beginners can progress faster and gain from general work.
  - Experienced athletes need more specific work and more time in Zone 3.
- **Age [B, L2575]:** the book says age may favour endurance goals, since endurance takes years to build. It says "after several years of strong peaks we will have to acknowledge" physical decline (L6480). It gives few age-specific rules [B?]. Masters racers are mentioned in Twight's essay (L1510).
- **Sex [B, L2277]:** women can train the same way. Women's pack loads are equal to men's, so strength-to-weight matters. Top women train at about 10% less volume only because of shorter events [B, L712].
- **Youth [B]:** adolescents or very weak athletes may benefit from a hypertrophy phase.
- **Beginners [B, L1037]:** train the base first. For less-experienced climbers the base may be the only block, and the book says it gives the best return.
- **Late-career or health conditions [I]:** the book does not cover them. Use clinician advice.

## 9. Questions to ask the athlete before planning

1. What is the goal (route, peak, expedition), and on what date? Is the date firm?
2. What technical level does the goal need? What can the athlete do now on rock, ice, mixed, and snow? Which skills are weak?
3. What is the goal's biggest day (vertical gain, hours, pack weight, altitude)?
4. How many hours did the athlete train each week over the last 12 months? What did they consist of?
5. How many long mountain days per month in the last year?
6. Any structured strength training in the last year? Current pull-up and one-leg step-up ability?
7. Any known maximum heart rate, a tested threshold, or a wearable? Which device and what data?
8. How does conversational-pace hiking feel? Does heart rate climb quickly at slow speeds?
9. Injury history, current pain, tendon problems, illness history, and medical conditions. Has a clinician cleared max testing and heavy lifting?
10. Weekly schedule: which days allow long sessions? Work, family, travel, shift work?
11. Access: steep hills, stairs, gym, climbing gym, rock, ice, snow, ski touring?
12. Age, sex, body weight, and recovery quality (sleep, stress, nutrition)?
13. Past peaks and crashes. Does the athlete tend to overtrain or undertrain?
14. Experience with altitude. Where, how high, how did acclimatisation go?
15. Food and drink habits on long days. Any digestive problems?
16. Motivation, fear, and team factors. Who is the partner, and how well-matched are they?
17. Does the athlete want only fitness advice, or also skill and risk-management guidance?

### 10.4 Interpretation cautions

- The book's methods rest on the authors' coaching experience and a selection of sport-science studies. Some claims (for example recovery tools, fasted training, and the neural account of strength reserve) are described by the authors as hypotheses or anecdotes.
- The book is about 2014 practice. HRV and wearable guidance is dated.
- The athletes in the examples are elite. Scale the examples to the athlete's base and time budget.
- Numbers in this file are paraphrased from the book. Check them against the cited lines before using them in client-facing material.
