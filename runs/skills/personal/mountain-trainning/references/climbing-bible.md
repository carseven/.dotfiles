Source file: `../the-climbing-bible.md` (Markdown conversion of an EPUB). This summary is a paraphrase for agent use. It is not a substitute for the book.

Legend used below:

- **[Book]** = content the book states (paraphrased). Numbers marked "(book)" come from the book.

## 1. Book identity, scope, how to use this file

**Identity [Book].** *The Climbing Bible: Practical Exercises* by Martin Mobråten and Stian Christophersen. English translation by Bjørn Sætnan. Vertebrate Publishing (Sheffield), English edition 2022. First published in Norwegian in 2020 as *Klatrebibelen Praktiske Øvelser*. Foreword by Cecilie Skog. The file also states that the main *The Climbing Bible* was first published in Norwegian in 2018 and in English in 2020. This book is the practical-exercises companion to it. It is not the main book.

**Authors [Book].** Mobråten: civil engineer, ex-national-team climber, ex-Nordic champion, coach of Norwegian youth recruits, route setter. Christophersen: physiotherapist and coach, ex-national-team coach, 2009 Norwegian bouldering champion, coach educator. Guest essays come from Martine Limstrand (trad/alpine) and Tina Johnsen Hafsaas (variation in training).

**Scope [Book].** Technique, strength and power, and exercises for children and youths. The authors state that endurance, flexibility, and the mental side are left out. They say the main *Climbing Bible* covers these. They suggest trying at least one new exercise per session.

**Scope consequences [Inference].** This file has no endurance, power-endurance, periodisation, weekly-programme, mobility, mental-training, or injury-rehabilitation content. Sections for these topics below say so and point to what little the book does say. Do not invent book support for them. Use the other books in this skill, or the main *Climbing Bible* (not available here), for those topics.

**How to use this file.** Read section 3 to find the topic. Use the "Agent application" bullets to choose exercises. Then use section 4 for athlete types and section 5 for intake questions. Quote exercise parameters only with attribution ("the book suggests…") and as a starting range. The authors say dosages are a guide and must be individualised. Always check section 6 before telling an athlete that the book "shows" a movement. The conversion has no images, and many exercises depend on photos.

## 3. Topics

### 3.1 Warm-up

**Core idea [Book].** A warm-up prepares body, technique, and mind, and lowers injury risk. It has a general part and a specific, climbing-based part. Adults and youths do the general part for at least 10 minutes (book). The specific part builds from easy climbing on good holds to harder climbing. The book wants over 100 moves before raising difficulty, to warm the fingers properly (book). It also wants the last part to match the main session (steep moves before steep bouldering; fingers and hip mobility before vertical leading).

**What the drills develop [Book].** The general part: skipping, running, stairs, burpees, then three mobility drills (cossack squats, standing rotations, thoracic bridge) with one set each per side in the book. The specific part adds: slow, full-range pull-ups (assisted); fast assisted power pull-ups; short deadhangs in varied grips (assisted); kick starts; and jumps to edges. The book places these before "most intensive sessions". Double dynos go at the end of the specific warm-up (see Dynamics).

**Agent application.**

- [Inference] Offer a warm-up template built from the book's structure: general (about 10 min), three mobility drills, 100+ easy-to-medium moves, then primers that match the session goal. Do not present primers as required for easy sessions. The book frames them for intensive sessions.
- [Book] Traversing is a good low-load way to log moves and practise clipping, footwork, resting and dynamics.
- [Inference] Scale the primers to the athlete. Beginners may skip jumps and kick starts until they control easy dynamic moves. Athletes with finger or shoulder symptoms should skip hangs and jumps until cleared.

**Common errors [Inference].** Skipping the specific part. Raising difficulty before 100 moves. Using loaded pull-ups or hangs as warm-up. The book tells you to unload so the primer stays a warm-up.

**Safety.** [Book] Pull-up and hang primers should be assisted (band or pulley). Hold the active shoulder position (shoulder blades down; elbows rotated slightly in).

**Source:** WARMING UP; THE WARM-UP PROGRAMME.

### 3.2 Technique: principles

**Core idea [Book].** Practise technique when rested and motivated, and before hard physical work. Pick a separate focus for each session. Work on weaknesses, but keep developing strengths. Watch others, film yourself, and discuss the exercise with a stronger partner or coach. Use low intensity for technique sessions; the authors say climbers wrongly try their hardest every session.

**Agent application.**

- [Inference] Plan technique blocks at the start of sessions, or as separate easy sessions, with one named drill and a reason for choosing it.
- [Book] Start with the easiest exercise in a section and work upward in difficulty.
- [Book] Use sidebars to justify variety: Johnny's Method (constraint climbing as a way to break habits) and Stian's story (training weaknesses can erode strengths, so keep a balance and separate "regular" climbing from weakness work).

**Common errors [Book].** Technique work while tired. Always climbing at limit. Training only the weaknesses.

**Source:** CHAPTER 1: TECHNIQUE (intro, Intensity); Johnny's Method; Moving slowly when you're good at moving fast!

### 3.3 Footwork

**Core idea [Book].** Legs should carry a large share of the load. That needs precise foot placement and a lowered body. Do not use worn-out shoes for footwork refinement. Soft shoes help quiet and precise placement; stiff shoes help high steps to small holds; aggressive downturned shoes help steep wall feet.

**Methods and what they train [Book].**

- Look up – look down: gaze moves to the foot or hand about to move; builds the habit of watching feet.
- Baby steps: three foot moves per hand move. Giant leaps (children's section) does the reverse.
- High steps: needs hip mobility; slopers limit the cheat of pushing hips out.
- Downclimbing: forces looking at feet; harder and pumpier than expected; partner belay is preferred to an auto-belay that pulls up.
- Ninja feet, sticky feet, chips only, use the tip: precision, no foot twisting, use of the shoe tip.
- Intermediate steps: smearing with a lowered heel; needs a wall with friction.
- Matching feet: three ways (side by side, sliding, small jump swap). Practise low on the bouldering wall with poorer handholds so arms cannot cheat.
- Strong feet and Trickster: keeping feet on steep terrain either by tension (toes like hooks, no heel/toe hooks) or by using hooks.

**Agent application [Inference].**

- Beginners and returners: look-down, baby steps, downclimbing, ninja feet, use the tip. Use routes well below limit.
- Intermediates: matching feet, intermediate steps, chips only, strong feet.
- Always begin on large footholds and progress to small; begin static, then add speed (book for Strong feet).

**Common errors [Book].** Standing on the middle of the shoe. Not looking at the foot. Raising the heel on smears. Starting at too hard a grade (downclimbing and looking-down drills get pumpy quickly).

**Safety.** [Book] Downclimbing needs belay routines. Use of heel/toe hooks and dynamic steep feet increases fall risk; start with short moves.

**Source:** FOOTWORK.

### 3.4 Grip positions

**Core idea [Book].** The book recognises open hand, pinch, half crimp, and crimp. Aggressive grips (half crimp, crimp) allow bigger, more explosive moves. Open hand suits precise climbing. Most climbers fall back on a preferred grip when pumped. Train all grips.

**Methods [Book].**

- Let the hold decide: repeat a known route using open hand on pockets and good holds, half crimp on edges, pinches on pinchable holds. A partner acts as judge.
- Practising crimps: stand on the ground with feet down, place the grip, bend knees slightly to load it. This is a safe introduction before use on the wall.

**Agent application [Inference].** Good for beginners who only use open hand. Move to loaded crimp work only after ground practice. Link to deadhangs for planned finger loading (section 3.9).

**Safety [Book].** The open-hand grip carries lower injury risk but limits climbing. The authors say to be careful with the full crimp and to avoid using it much in training.

**Source:** GRIP POSITIONS.

### 3.5 Balance

**Core idea [Book].** Balance saves energy. Balance means the hips are centred over the support area. Wide foot placement helps. Train on slabs, vertical, or slightly overhanging walls with poor handholds, often slopers.

**Methods [Book].** Weight distribution (shift the body, find the balance point, then move the hand). Traversing (include in every warm-up). Locking off 1 to 3 seconds before each hold (book). Matching every hold (and the contrasting "no matching"). Find the rest (book: on routes 2 to 3 grades below max, look for five rests of 20 to 30 seconds; a good rest allows a straight arm on open hand). Climbing without pinching ("technique killer"). Fewer fingers (three, then two fingers on each hold). One hand only. No hands. These constraints reduce arm pulling and make the feet and hips do the work.

**Agent application [Inference].**

- Use "find the rest" as the nearest book item to endurance training. It trains recovery skill on routes. It is not an endurance programme.
- Constraint drills (few fingers, one hand, no hands) suit vertical and slab terrain. Keep the moves short and holds large for the extreme variants.
- Alpine-oriented athletes: slab climbing with two or three fingers aligns with Limstrand's advice (section 3.12).

**Common errors [Book].** Pulling with arms when the hold "feels good". Pinching to lean out. Skipping the balance search before moving the hand.

**Safety [Book].** Do few-finger work on slab or vertical to limit finger load.

**Source:** BALANCE.

### 3.6 Tension, direction of force, compression

**Core idea [Book].** Position the body so each hold works in its best direction (sidepulls, undercuts, opposing surfaces). These drills resemble balance drills, but are steeper and about push/pull direction.

**Methods [Book].** Finding the position (full control, no kipping). Sidepulls only (then without pinching). Crossing the line (crossing hands past the centre line, then long crosses, then opposing sidepulls). Compression on prows with three arm positions. The book says you are strongest with hands wide and at shoulder height and with symmetric positions. The "T-rex" position (hands close together near the centre line) is weak.

**Agent application [Inference].** Useful for climbers who "muscle" steep walls or fail on sidepulls and prows. Use easy routes so there is strength left for good style.

**Common errors [Book].** Dynamic shortcuts that hide poor positioning. Always using the T-rex position.

**Source:** TENSION & DIRECTION OF FORCE (including the twisting sidebar).

### 3.7 Dynamics and tricks

**Core idea [Book].** Dynamic movement uses leg drive and a controlled arc: body moves out slightly, then in, and reaches the hold near the top of its path, hips close to the wall. A dynamic move is not the same as a dyno. The authors note a mental barrier to letting go. Start with slow, low-speed exercises to build confidence.

**Methods [Book].**

- Wall bars ladder: one hand, then both hands, then clapping, then sideways targets. Wall bars are the safest first step.
- Down and up: deadpoint on a slightly overhanging wall with good holds.
- Deadly precision: repeat one move at least 10 more times (book) after first success. Check quality by how much force you use to stop at the hold. Then restrict grips, then worsen the start holds (guide-hold and opposed-sloper variants).
- Dynos: single and double; overshoot speed slightly so the elbows are bent on landing. Double dynos go at the end of the specific warm-up (book).
- Arching and cross moves: shift sideways first; on dynamic crosses with feet off, open the hips and front the wall rather than twist in.
- Tricks (flick, cross clutch, double clutch, wall runs): modern competition-style skills. Start on good holds, short moves, easier walls. The double-clutch guidance is 20 to 40 degrees overhanging (book).
- Combination exercises: static vs dynamic, 5 problems in 5 minutes (one per angle), copycat (copy a different-style partner), problem-solving (five new problems in 20 minutes).

**Agent application [Inference].** Tricks mostly suit gym boulderers and competition climbers. They have little relevance to trad or alpine goals. The combination drills help any climber who is stuck in one style.

**Common errors [Book].** Trying two-finger grabs before the move is learnt (forearm strain risk, book). Grabbing with the hips far from the wall. Stopping mid-movement.

**Safety [Book].** Build up from low-speed, low-height moves. Increase distance and steepness after confidence grows.

**Source:** DYNAMICS; TRICKS; COMBINATION EXERCISES.

### 3.8 Wall-based strength and power

**Core idea [Book].** Training on the wall keeps all movement components in the exercise. Finger strength relative to body weight is described as probably the most important physical factor. The number of reps matters little as long as the set tires you. Choose the dosage by goal: maximum strength means fewer reps near maximal effort. A general base means more volume. The closer to maximum effort, the longer the rest. Power means force times velocity: move at maximum speed and use maximum effort on the landing, with less load than strength work.

**Variables for selecting problems [Book].** Grip type, move length, wall angle. Small edges or pinches train fingers. Poor holds that stop you releasing tension train compression. Good holds without matching train pulling through. Steeper walls increase arm, body and core demand. For less experienced climbers the book warns that finger training on walls steeper than about 30 degrees needs fairly good holds. Use relatively equal holds and equal spacing when you set your own problems. Know the problems in advance, so attempts are high quality.

**Methods and their target [Book].** Each is a bouldering-style set structure with a stated move count, tries, rest, angle, and hold type. The book's common pattern is 5 to 8 moves, about 15 tries total (about 10 for harder variants), and at least 3 minutes of rest (4 minutes for limit work).

- Finger strength: Mouse moves, Themed bouldering (edge/pinch/sloper/pocket themes, varied to limit uniform loading).
- Capacity: 4 x 4 (a circuit with short 60 s rests; the book says to pick problems with a margin).
- Body strength: Feet on the wall (40 degrees or steeper), Campusing (needs about five pull-ups first, book), Compression, Pulling through.
- Positioning: Fronting, Twisting in, Gaston, Locking off (2 to 3 s), Turtle (steady speed), Marking (feet touch next hold), Hang on (stop the swing, replace the feet).
- Limit and style: On the limit, American style (big dynamic moves, bent-arm landings).
- Power: Jump start and kick start (hold a deliberately poor landing for 2 to 3 s; the book gives low rep counts such as 9 per hand across three problems and short rests between jumps). Dyno variants: Three points off, All points off, Kangaroo, Monkey (steeper, longer-rest structure). Campus Go big and Go fast (stop the set when speed drops).

**Agent application [Inference].**

- Choose by the athlete's limiter. Fingers weak: small-hold, short-move sets on moderate angles. Body tension weak: steeper walls with good holds. Slow/static weak: lock-offs, turtle. Fast/dynamic weak: power section.
- Order inside a session: warm-up, technique, power, strength, then any volume. The book states technique comes before hard physical work and campus work goes early. A power-before-strength order is my inference.
- Rest is a quality control. Stian's own advice: ask "am I ready for another go?", then rest another minute or two (book).
- Frequency and weekly loading are not given. Do not invent them from this book.

**Common errors [Book].** Cutting rests short. Training weaknesses so much that strengths decay (Stian's story). Using problems that are too hard to execute well.

**Safety [Book].** Start campus moves short on good holds with slightly bent elbows. Jump starts use landing holds poor enough that you only just stop the momentum, so add difficulty gradually. Avoid small positive holds for youth wall exercises (book, children's strength section).

**Source:** CHAPTER 2 intro; EXERCISES ON THE WALL; IMPORTANT CONSIDERATIONS…; STRENGTH EXERCISES; POSITIONING EXERCISES; POWER EXERCISES.

### 3.9 Finger strength (isolated)

**Core idea [Book].** Deadhangs are described as the most specific and controllable finger-strength method. The authors argue the risk comes from dosage and method, not from hanging itself. Done in a controlled way, they say it carries lower injury risk than a normal climbing session. They argue systematic finger training strengthens muscle, tendon, joint and ligament over time. The default grip is the half crimp if only one grip is trained. Vary grips so open hand, slopers, and pockets also improve.

**Unloading [Book].** Anyone can hang, by taking weight off with a pulley and harness, or a band. The book's example is a 70 kg climber unloading 35 kg. Reduce the unloading when each hang has a clear margin. Keep shoulders down and elbows slightly turned in.

**Methods [Book] (ranges only; see the file for full parameters).**

| Method | Intent | Book's dosage in brief |
|---|---|---|
| Torture hangs | Local endurance of fingers | 30 to 40 s hangs, 4 to 6 sets, 2 to 3 min rest, no margin |
| Repeaters 1 | Varied grips, near failure on the last hang | 7 s on / 3 s off x 7, 4 to 5 grips, 2 to 3 min rest |
| Repeaters 2 | Harder, one grip | 10 s on / 5 s off x 4, 4 sets, 3 to 5 min rest |
| Maximum weight | Max strength on deep edges | 10 s hangs, 5 sets, 3 min rest, 1 to 3 s margin |
| Shallow edge | Strength via smaller edge | same pattern; go shallower if hangs exceed about 13 s |
| Short and hard | Rate of force development | 3 to 5 s, 6 sets, 1 min rest, pull hard |
| One-hand hang | Advanced progression | Only after lots of two-hand hanging; use unloading; stop body rotation with the free hand |

**Portable fingerboard [Book].** Hangs work, but boards move. Alternatives: hang weights from the board and lift them, one hand at a time. Finger curls (open hand to crimp) build dynamic finger strength. The book gives 8 curls per set, 3 sets per hand, 3 min rest, 1 rep in reserve, and says to start lighter than expected.

**Jump to the edge on the campus board [Book].** Fast gripping and stopping momentum. Hold the landing 2 s; the book lists three versions with six to eight reps.

**Agent application [Inference].**

- Prerequisite: a warm-up (section 3.1), good technique, no active finger pain, adult or fully grown finger bones (book for campus; see 3.11 for youth).
- Select by goal: capacity (torture, repeaters) vs maximum strength (max weight, shallow edge, short and hard). Do not stack all methods in one block; the book says to vary methods for progression and variety.
- Progress by margin: when the athlete holds well beyond the target time, reduce unloading or use a poorer hold. Margin definition: how much longer the athlete could have hung (book footnote).
- Weekly frequency, total finger volume per week, and block length are not specified.

**Common errors.** [Book] Too much dosage. Poor shoulder position. [Inference] Using the full crimp. Chasing numbers with no margin on every set.

**Safety [Book].** Do the specific finger work with control and respect dosage. The book says young climbers whose fingers have not finished growing need great care (section 3.11). Add load only from relatively deep edges (book, Maximum weight).

**Source:** ISOLATED STRENGTH EXERCISES → DEADHANGS; EXERCISES USING A PORTABLE FINGERBOARD.

### 3.10 Arm, upper body, core, and campus board

**Core idea [Book].** Climbing is pulling-based, but the authors plan most arm and upper-body work on the wall. Isolated work is a supplement.

**Methods [Book].**

- Lock-offs: two-handed (work toward 30 s; deload to about 10 s first), one-handed torture hangs (15 s), one-handed max hangs (3 to 5 s), jump to lock-off. Vary elbow angles from 90 degrees to nearly straight.
- Pull-ups: regular (8 to 12 reps x 3, calm), weighted (4 x 4 at above 85 percent of 1RM or at most 1 rep in reserve), jerks and clap (speed; stop when speed drops), negatives (eccentric; severe soreness risk; the book's example is 1 to 2 reps x 3 once weekly rising to 8 reps x 3 twice weekly), archer, one-arm (learn with unloading, ideally a pulley).
- Combinations: fingerboard pull-ups on poor holds; offset pull-ups (hands at different heights, matching the grip to the target).
- Campus: strength session (good rungs, calm, short ladders with 60 s rests), power session (maximum speed and distance, singles and doubles). Learn moves with feet on footholds below the board first.
- Front lever: progress from quarter to full by hang time; move up when you hold 15 s. 5 to 15 s x 4 sets (book). Front lever pull-ups are for the strongest, in quarter/half/tuck variations.

**Agent application [Inference].**

- Prerequisites: about five pull-ups before campus (book). Pull-up and lock-off work suits athletes who fail at pulling through, locking, or steep terrain. Keep the load progression via unloading before weight.
- Campus is optional. For most recreational, time-limited, or alpine-oriented athletes the book's wall exercises are a safer substitute.
- RIR is the book's intensity tool (book footnote: for strength to matter, RIR should not exceed 5). Use RIR when 1RM is unknown.

**Common errors.** [Book] Skipping deloading when learning a technique. Letting the body rotate on one-arm hangs. [Inference] Adding weight before the athlete holds the target time with margin.

**Safety [Book].** Campus: only fully grown, experienced climbers; double dynos only for those who have done systematic lower-level campus work. Do campus early, when warm and fresh, before regular climbing. Negatives can cause severe soreness. Use RIR or deload instead of grinding.

**Source:** ARM & UPPER BODY STRENGTH EXERCISES.

### 3.11 Children and youths

**Core idea [Book].** The chapter is for coaches. Warm-up must be longer and playful because children do not show the adult physiological pre-activity changes. Games use technique themes (footwork, balance, precision, cooperation). Strength training is described as safe and useful from about age five or six if the child is motivated and can follow instructions (book). The main aim is health and robustness, not results. The authors dispute the myth that strength training harms growth zones; they say excessive dosage is the risk.

**Key points [Book].**

- Child warm-up length: the book says 20 minutes in the warm-up chapter and 30 to 35 minutes in the children's chapter. This is an internal inconsistency. [Inference] Use "20 to 35 minutes, play-based, in short bursts" and check the conflict with the user.
- Child strength sessions: 15 to 20 minutes, varied, multi-part, dynamic bodyweight work.
- Base exercises: push-ups, assisted pull-ups, sit-ups, squats, hanging leg lifts. Pair exercises: wheelbarrow, partner push-ups, balance and push games.
- Early puberty: add quick direction changes and plyometrics (burpees, jump squats) and wall exercises from Chapter 2 (but avoid small positive holds).
- Boys and girls respond differently to strength training; girls need slower load and volume progression (book).
- Body weight changes in puberty raise finger load; reduce or individualise finger work during rapid growth.
- Deadhangs for youth (book "appendix", which relaxes the main book's ban): active hang, 15 to 20 s with a 3 to 5 s margin, 3 to 4 sets, 2 to 3 min rest. One-hand lock-offs up to 15 s. The book still discourages campus boarding and finger-only training for young athletes. It asks that finger symptoms be taken seriously and medically checked.
- Coaching style [Book]: reward progress and effort (mastery), let children solve moves, avoid too much early feedback.

**Agent application [Inference].** If the athlete is a parent or coach, use this chapter. If the athlete is a minor, keep the agent's role to general guidance and recommend a qualified coach and parental involvement. Do not prescribe finger loading for minors beyond the book's cautious ranges, and note that these are adult-reader-facing coach guides.

**Source:** CHAPTER 3: CHILDREN & YOUTHS (all subsections).

### 3.12 Training for trad and alpine

**Core idea [Book, Limstrand essay, short].** Gear skills, mental skills, and equipment cannot be fully practised indoors. Still, better climbing skills can improve trad confidence. Endurance helps because alpine pitches can take over half an hour and the same hand may hold for long periods while placing gear. Indoor work should improve endurance and lock-off ability. Long approaches need leg strength and endurance. Good technique shifts load to the legs. She advises trusting bad footholds (e.g. slabs with two or three fingers). Finger strength and slab climbing are called more relevant than steep bouldering on big holds. Indoor cracks, if available, allow jamming practice in winter.

**Agent application [Inference].** The essay is the only alpine content. It gives priorities, not a programme. Pair it with: footwork drills (3.3), balance and rest-finding (3.5), few-finger slab work, finger strength (3.9), lock-offs (3.10). For aerobic and leg work, use the other books in this skill. Do not suggest that this book prepares an athlete for gear placement, fall judgment, or route-finding.

**Source:** TRAINING FOR TRAD & ALPINE – by Martine Limstrand.

### 3.13 Variation, individualisation, and mindset (essays)

**[Book]** Tina Johnsen Hafsaas describes shifting from technical crimpy climbing to steeper, harder, more physical climbing, then back to technical bouldering to cover a new weakness. She also describes the value of a coach as a sparring partner and the value of curiosity. Foreword and commandments emphasise progression, community, joy, and getting rid of fear of falling.

**Agent application [Inference].** Use these for motivation and for "why vary?" questions. Do not treat them as methods. The book has no fall-practice or mental-skills programme, even though Commandments 5 and 8 name fear of falling and mindset (the book defers the mental topic to the main *Climbing Bible*).

**Source:** VARIATION IN TRAINING; HOOKED; THE 10 COMMANDMENTS OF CLIMBING; INTRODUCTION.

General rules for choosing exercises [I]:

1. Start from the athlete's limiter and goal, then pick the exercise whose target matches.
2. Technique before physical work in each session; power before strength; volume last.
3. One new exercise per session at most, then repeat to learn it.
4. Progress by making holds poorer, moves longer, or angle steeper, one variable at a time (book describes these levers; the one-at-a-time rule is my inference).

## 5. Questions to ask the athlete

1. How old are you, and are you fully grown? (Campus and finger loading depend on this.)
2. How long have you climbed, and how many sessions per week can you keep?
3. What is your goal: indoor bouldering, sport leading, trad, alpine, or general fitness? What grade and style?
4. What are your strengths and weaknesses? Which holds, angles, or move types make you fall?
5. Do you have any finger, elbow, shoulder, or other pain now or in the past year? Has a clinician cleared you?
6. Have you done deadhangs, fingerboard, campus, or weighted pull-ups before? How did they go?
7. What equipment can you use: boulder wall, training wall with open setting, wall bars, campus board, fingerboard, pulley, bands, weights?
8. How many pull-ups can you do? Can you hang on half crimp, and for how long, with what unloading?
9. Which shoes do you use, and what is their condition?
10. Do you have a partner or coach to film, judge, or belay?
11. How do you warm up now? Do you reach 100 moves before going hard?
12. Are you training for a trip or event with a date? What other training (running, strength) competes for recovery?
13. For a youth athlete: what is the biological and training age, and has there been a recent growth spurt?
14. How do you feel about falling and dynamic moves? (The book does not teach falling; refer to the main *Climbing Bible* or a coach.)
