# Cross-book synthesis

Read this file first when a plan combines climbing, aerobic work, and bodyweight strength. It compares the four books, shows where each is silent, and states how to combine them. The per-book files hold the detail.

## 3. Principles all four books share

1. **Progress gradually.** Raise load in small steps and allow adaptation. New Alpinism calls it gradualness and continuity. Overcoming Gravity applies progressive overload and warns that muscle strength outruns tendon tolerance. Uphill Athlete limits weekly volume jumps. [B]
2. **Specificity grows with the athlete.** Beginners benefit from general work. Experienced athletes need work that matches the goal's demands. [B]
3. **Recovery is part of training.** Adaptation happens between sessions. Both House–Johnston books give monitoring tools and rules for cutting load. [B]
4. **Individualise.** Every book states that its numbers are guides, not prescriptions. [B]
5. **Quality of movement first.** The Climbing Bible puts technique practice ahead of fatigue. Overcoming Gravity demands clean form before progression. [B]
6. **Balance.** Overcoming Gravity stresses push–pull balance. Climbing training adds antagonist work. The aerobic books ask for strength and mobility to protect the athlete. [B]

## 4. Where the books differ or leave a gap

- **Zone model.** New Alpinism defines zones as percentages of maximum heart rate, with Zone 1 as the base zone and Zone 2 as a low-value middle zone. Uphill Athlete anchors zones to AeT and LT, and its Zone 3–5 percentages come from a chart missing in the conversion. Both agree that most volume stays low and easy. [B] [I] Do not mix the two models in one plan. Pick the model that matches the athlete's measurements and name it in the plan.
- **Intensity gate.** Both books use a rule that compares the aerobic and anaerobic thresholds before adding harder work (a gap of about 10 percent). Details and examples differ. [B] Use the version in the file you rely on.
- **Testing heart rate.** New Alpinism describes a max-HR field test and rejects the "220 minus age" formula. Uphill Athlete does not give a max-HR test. It gives AeT tests instead, from an age-based formula to lactate and lab tests, and it calls the formula conservative. [B] [I] Offer a medical check before any maximal test for older or at-risk athletes.
- **Strength dosing.** The aerobic books use gym strength as a supporting element and then ME work. Overcoming Gravity treats bodyweight strength as a primary goal with many skill progressions. [B] [I] For a mountain athlete, use Overcoming Gravity for exercise choice and progression, and the aerobic books for how much strength fits around endurance.
- **Finger strength.** The Climbing Bible and the Padilla course cover it. Overcoming Gravity does not. [B] They disagree on who may start hangs. The Climbing Bible allows unloaded hangs for adults. One Padilla video asks for 1–2 years of systematic climbing first, and another allows bodyweight hangs after 6–8 months. [I] For a climber with under one year of experience, plan around the stricter rule: wall-based finger work first, and hangs only after technique and training frequency meet the course criteria, or after a coach agrees. Do not infer finger prescriptions from Overcoming Gravity.
- **Climbing endurance.** The Climbing Bible companion omits endurance and periodisation. [B] Use New Alpinism for the big-picture structure and treat climbing-specific endurance sessions as [I].
- **Conflicts inside books.** Each per-book file lists internal contradictions in its numbers (for example warm-up durations, hill-sprint lengths, and tendon-return rates). Mention uncertainty when you quote them.

## 5. Combining the three training types [I]

The books do not provide one shared plan. Use these rules as defaults, then adapt.

1. **Fix the goal and date.** Climbing grade, alpine objective, race, or general fitness. Choose the primary quality: aerobic endurance, climbing performance, or strength.
2. **Count the athlete's real weekly capacity.** Use recent training, not an ideal. Ask for hours, days, and recovery.
3. **Give the primary quality the most time and the freshest days.** Keep the others at maintenance dose when the primary quality needs the recovery.
4. **Limit hard days.** Treat hard climbing, heavy strength, and high-intensity aerobic work as hard days. Aerobic base sessions at easy effort are not hard days. [I] New Alpinism uses general phases (transition, base, strength, ME, specific, peak) that you can map onto a calendar.
5. **Order within a day.** Technique and finger work come first when fresh. Do strength before easy aerobic work when both happen. [I] Avoid hard legs before technical climbing.
6. **Reduce on schedule.** Use planned lighter weeks, and cut load after poor sleep, illness, pain, or low motivation, as the monitoring sections describe.
7. **Review often.** Compare plan to actual training, then adjust the next block. Do not assume missed sessions happened.

### Competing goals: plan in blocks [I]

Athletes rarely maximise two demanding goals at once. Most split the year into blocks of about 8–12 weeks.

- **One main goal per block.** It gets most of the hard sessions. The other goal gets a maintenance dose. Then the blocks swap.
- **Maintenance dose (estimate, not from the books).** About one easy run a week of 30–60 min keeps an aerobic base. About one climbing session a week holds a climbing level.
- **Hard-day budget.** A week holds about 3 hard days. Hard running, hard climbing, finger work, and heavy legs share that budget. Zone 2 running is not a hard day.
- **Interference.** Easy Zone 2 running interferes little with climbing and strength. Hard intervals on the same day as heavy legs or finger work interfere more.
- **Climbing level.** For a climber near V2–V3, most gains come from climbing volume, technique, and later finger strength. Running adds little to the grade, so climbing usually goes first.
- **Race target in a later block.** A road-race pace target (for example 10 km at 5:30/km) needs at least three runs a week. Plan it as its own block. In that block, drop climbing to 1–2 maintenance sessions.
- **Say the trade-off to the athlete.** If they want both at once, expect slower progress on each.

## 6. Safety rules for any plan

- This skill gives education, not diagnosis or medical clearance. Ask about injuries, symptoms, and medical restrictions first.
- Stop and seek urgent help for chest pain, fainting, or severe breathlessness. [I]
- Stop an exercise that causes pain, tingling, or worsening symptoms. Refer persistent symptoms to a qualified clinician. [B] for the principle (Overcoming Gravity, injury sections). [I] for the specific triggers.
- Fitness does not replace climbing instruction, protection skills, equipment knowledge, or route judgment. [B] (Climbing Bible, trad and alpine chapter)
- Treat fasted, low-carbohydrate, altitude, and maximal-test advice as options that need individual suitability checks. [I]

## 7. Citing and handling sources

- Cite the book and section for every book-based recommendation. Use the section names in the per-book files, not the conversion's generic "Chapter N" headings.
- Give approximate numbers as the book's guidance, not as an exact prescription for this athlete.
- Summarise in your own words. Do not reproduce long passages from the books or the conversions.
- State when a figure, table, or video is missing from the conversion.
